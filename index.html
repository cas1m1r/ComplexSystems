Understanding Complex Systems

From shared state to agreement

An explanation-first course in concurrency, ownership, and distributed uncertainty
Expanded textbook edition · Revision 0.3 · 24 October 2026

This book follows the questions that forced each new mechanism to become necessary. It begins with two threads and an incorrect counter, then develops mutual exclusion, conditional claims, cache contention, handoffs, publication, versioned state, and quorum reasoning. It ends where the coursework currently stands: independent nodes, stale leadership, and a nonblocking socket server whose connection lifecycle must be made explicit.

The organizing question is:

What is the system entitled to believe, and what mechanism establishes that belief?

You do not need the original chats to follow the text. The discussion has been rewritten as a continuous explanation, retaining the misconceptions, corrections, and worked traces that made the ideas understandable. Discussion questions are condensed from the source rather than presented as a verbatim transcript. New controlled experiments are labeled separately.

How to work through the book

Assume basic C functions, loops, pointers, and structures; the concurrency concepts are developed from the beginning. Linux or WSL with a C11 compiler is the reference execution environment. A short example can be saved using its displayed filename and built with:

gcc -std=c11 -O2 -Wall -Wextra -pthread example.c -o example
./example

The assembly probe deliberately has no main; its chapter uses -c or -S. The two ordinary racy counter demonstrations contain undefined behavior and are identified as such. The teaching programs otherwise focus on the stated protocol and usually assume successful pthread initialization and creation; they are not complete production frameworks.

For each lab, first predict the result, then draw a permitted execution, then run it. Keep the invariant separate from a particular winning thread or a particular timing. Use the guided answers to check the reasoning, not only the output. A failed speculative attempt can be how correctness is preserved.

The sixteen submitted C files are indexed in Appendix A and preserved in the companion archive. Complete teaching versions are inline, so the Markdown and offline HTML remain readable without opening external code files. The source captures and exact continuation marker are retained for later additions.

1. Correctness under assumptions

The course began with a precise goal: understand how systems remain correct, safe, or recoverable when their assumptions fail. Concurrency, consensus, security, and formal methods are different settings for that same inquiry.

An execution is a sequence of state transitions. A concurrent program admits many executions because thread operations can overlap and interleave. Correctness has to cover every execution permitted by the model, including ones that never appeared in the few runs we observed.

An invariant must hold throughout the relevant execution or observation boundary. A postcondition is required after an operation or computation finishes. For a counter, counter == 2 * ITERATIONS after both workers join is a postcondition; it is not something that should hold throughout their loops. Always specify when a relationship must be observable as true.

Term Question to ask
State What information affects the next decision?
Transition What change is being attempted?
Invariant What must remain true at the specified boundary?
Safety What bad outcome must never be accepted?
Liveness What useful outcome must eventually occur, under which assumptions?
Atomicity Exactly which operation is indivisible to the relevant observers?
Ownership Who has permission to access or change this state?
Evidence What establishes that a decision is justified?

“Atomic” does not mean one clock cycle, nor does it make surrounding code atomic. Its boundary could be an increment, a conditional claim, or a larger operation implemented by a protocol.

The recurring lesson template

For each experiment, record:

  1. Invariant: the property and its observation boundary.
  2. Mechanism: the rule intended to preserve it.
  3. Assumptions: participant behavior and environmental guarantees.
  4. Failure: the execution that violates an assumption or property.
  5. Detection: what evidence exposes the failure, and what remains uncertain.
  6. Containment: what prevents one failure from spreading.
  7. Recovery: how the system returns to useful valid operation.
  8. Adversarial angle: whether timing or another assumption can be manipulated.
  9. Lesson: what the next design must establish explicitly.

The initial roadmap included independent replicated processes, crash recovery, network partitions, Raft, Byzantine protocols, model checking, and fault injection. Those remain a roadmap, not claims of completed implementations. This edition covers the actual C progression and its connections through the one-slot replica simulator and the unfinished split-vote exercise.

Why “it worked” is an incomplete observation

Suppose the program ends with the expected number. There are at least three possible explanations: the mechanism prevented an incorrect execution; the scheduler happened not to expose the defect; or the test did not inspect the property that was actually violated. Those explanations demand different next steps. More successful runs cannot, by themselves, distinguish all of them.

The first question in a useful investigation is therefore not which library to use. It is what the program claims. “This program is thread-safe” is too broad to test. “No ticket ID is assigned to two workers” is specific. “After all workers finish, issued tickets plus remaining inventory equals the initial inventory” is another claim. A mechanism might establish one without making an arbitrary intermediate read of both counters consistent.

The word always needs a scope. If a transfer first subtracts from checking and then adds to savings, memory can contain an intermediate total of 900. A design may permit that representation while ensuring that no cooperating reader accepts it as a completed account state. Its invariant is about accepted snapshots, not every internal machine instruction. Conversely, if negative inventory may trigger some independent action, saying that a later rollback repairs the count may be too weak: the forbidden intermediate state was already observable to the actor that mattered.

A discussion that explains the course's method

Reader, condensed: Are we studying several unrelated techniques, or one underlying problem?

Explanation: The techniques supply different kinds of evidence. A mutex can establish exclusive access to shared state. A version check can establish that the state did not change during a particular read. A quorum can establish that enough distinct participants endorsed a decision. Each conclusion is conditional on a different set of assumptions.

An assumption is not an embarrassment to hide after describing a guarantee. It tells the reader where the guarantee comes from. The counter mutex relies on all writers using the same lock. The versioned snapshot relies on writers advancing the version correctly. A three-vote certificate relies on distinct replica identities and appropriate voting rules. Replacing a cooperative writer with an arbitrary faulty actor changes the model; it does not make the earlier proof mysteriously wrong.

A failure trace is useful because it connects a broken assumption to a specific forbidden transition. “There is a race” names a class of defect. “A and B both read one remaining seat before either claims it” explains this one. We will repeatedly turn that sentence into a small table, then move the synchronization boundary until the forbidden trace is excluded.

Reconstruct the argument

For any lab, write three sentences before running it: the result that would count as a failure; the rule that supposedly excludes that failure; and the participant or environmental behavior on which the rule depends. Then ask which of those sentences the output actually checks. This is the habit that makes the later move from threads to independent machines possible.

2. Lab: the counter and the lock that was not shared

Two workers each increment a counter one million times. After both finish, the intended result is two million.

A source-level counter++ looks like one action, but a useful conceptual breakdown is read, compute, then write. Both workers can read the same old value and overwrite one another's result.

Step Worker A Worker B
Read Sees 0 Sees 0
Compute Computes 1 Computes 1
Write Stores 1 Stores 1

This is an explanatory failure trace, not a complete specification of the broken C program. Ordinary unsynchronized conflicting accesses create a C data race, which causes undefined behavior. C does not promise merely a smaller final count.

A broader race condition is a timing-dependent logical failure; one can exist even when all individual accesses use atomics. A nondeterministic winner, by itself, need not be a bug.

The initial program — intentionally undefined

Edited source listing — counter_racy.c: compact formatting and an explicit unused-argument annotation; the deliberate data race remains.

#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdio.h>

#ifndef ITERATIONS
#define ITERATIONS 1000000L
#endif

long counter = 0;

void *worker(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    for (long i = 0; i < ITERATIONS; i++) {
        counter++;
    }
    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t a, b;
    pthread_create(&a, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_create(&b, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_join(a, NULL);
    pthread_join(b, NULL);
    printf("expected=%ld actual=%ld\n", 2L * ITERATIONS, counter);
    return 0;
}

Compile this particular demonstration with -O0 first:

gcc -std=c11 -O0 -Wall -Wextra -pthread counter_racy.c -o counter_racy
./counter_racy

A wrong total exposes a symptom. A correct total does not establish safety. Changing optimization may dramatically alter the symptom because the program already has undefined behavior. volatile is not a repair.

The first attempted repair: one private mutex per worker

The learner correctly suspected the lock's scope when their mutex version still failed. A mutex declared inside worker is a different object in each invocation. Both workers can own their respective locks simultaneously.

Deliberately broken reconstruction — counter_private_mutex.c:

#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdio.h>

#ifndef ITERATIONS
#define ITERATIONS 1000000L
#endif

long counter = 0;

void *worker(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    pthread_mutex_t lock = PTHREAD_MUTEX_INITIALIZER; // Different object per worker
    for (long i = 0; i < ITERATIONS; i++) {
        pthread_mutex_lock(&lock);
        counter++; // Still a data race: workers use different locks
        pthread_mutex_unlock(&lock);
    }
    pthread_mutex_destroy(&lock);
    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t a, b;
    pthread_create(&a, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_create(&b, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_join(a, NULL);
    pthread_join(b, NULL);
    printf("expected=%ld actual=%ld\n", 2L * ITERATIONS, counter);
    return 0;
}

The shared resource is the counter, but there is no common coordination object. Each worker has protected itself from itself. This example still has undefined behavior; the presence of mutex calls is not enough.

Correct repair A: one shared mutex

Edited source listing — counter_mutex.c:

#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdio.h>

#ifndef ITERATIONS
#define ITERATIONS 1000000L
#endif

long counter = 0;
pthread_mutex_t lock = PTHREAD_MUTEX_INITIALIZER;

void *worker(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    for (long i = 0; i < ITERATIONS; i++) {
        pthread_mutex_lock(&lock);
        counter++;
        pthread_mutex_unlock(&lock);
    }
    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t a, b;
    pthread_create(&a, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_create(&b, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_join(a, NULL);
    pthread_join(b, NULL);
    printf("expected=%ld actual=%ld\n", 2L * ITERATIONS, counter);
    pthread_mutex_destroy(&lock);
    return 0;
}

This does not require the counter itself to be atomic. All concurrent accesses to it occur under the same mutex, and main reads it after both joins. The mutex supplies mutual exclusion and the necessary synchronization.

Correct repair B: an atomic read-modify-write

Consolidated source listing — counter_atomic.c:

#include <stdatomic.h>
#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdio.h>

#ifndef ITERATIONS
#define ITERATIONS 1000000L
#endif

atomic_long counter = 0;

void *worker(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    for (long i = 0; i < ITERATIONS; i++) {
        atomic_fetch_add(&counter, 1);
    }
    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t a, b;
    pthread_create(&a, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_create(&b, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_join(a, NULL);
    pthread_join(b, NULL);
    printf("expected=%ld actual=%ld\n", 2L * ITERATIONS, atomic_load(&counter));
    return 0;
}

atomic_fetch_add makes the increment an indivisible read-modify-write on that object. It does not lock an arbitrary surrounding region of code.

What changed? The mutex version protects an agreed critical section; the atomic version gives the particular increment its required semantics. Both can preserve the same final-count property.

Try: identify which object is shared in each listing. Explain why moving a mutex declaration changes correctness while changing a variable's cache-line placement, by itself, does not repair this race.

Why the private-mutex repair looks convincing

The attempted worker visibly contains lock, counter++, and unlock. A reader following source indentation can therefore feel that the increment is enclosed by protection. But mutex ownership belongs to an object, not to a source-code location. Two calls to the same function can execute identical source while operating on different stack objects.

Name the objects explicitly. Worker A creates lock_A; worker B creates lock_B. A acquiring lock_A says nothing about whether B owns lock_B. There is no conflict in the locking mechanism, so both increments can overlap. The repair is to share the coordination object because the protected resource is shared. A function-local static lock could also be shared; “must be a global variable” is not the fundamental rule. The requirement is common identity, suitable lifetime, and consistent participation.

Reader, condensed: I nearly put the lock beside the counter originally. Does that mean a mutex and an atomic are doing the same thing underneath?

Explanation: They can preserve the same abstract increment while offering different interfaces. A mutex says who may execute a region. An atomic read-modify-write defines one object operation. Lock implementations commonly use atomic primitives, but a call to atomic_fetch_add is not a request to acquire a pthread mutex around whatever comes next.

Separate the language defect from the algorithmic defect

The familiar read/add/write trace explains lost updates, but the ordinary racy C program has a stronger problem: its accesses are not ordered as C requires. It would be misleading to teach the smaller count as its only possible result. Even choosing -O0 does not restore a language guarantee; it merely makes some implementation behavior easier to inspect.

We can isolate the algorithmic defect with a second experiment that uses only atomic accesses. Each worker atomically loads zero. A barrier makes both loads happen before either worker's store. Both then atomically store one. Every individual access is defined and sequentially consistent, yet two logical increments leave the counter at one.

Additional controlled experiment — counter_atomic_split.c:

#define _POSIX_C_SOURCE 200809L
#include <assert.h>
#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdatomic.h>
#include <stdio.h>

atomic_int counter = 0;
pthread_barrier_t after_load;

void *worker(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    int observed = atomic_load(&counter);
    pthread_barrier_wait(&after_load);
    atomic_store(&counter, observed + 1);
    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t a, b;
    pthread_barrier_init(&after_load, NULL, 2);
    pthread_create(&a, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_create(&b, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_join(a, NULL);
    pthread_join(b, NULL);
    printf("logical increments=2 final counter=%d\n", atomic_load(&counter));
    assert(atomic_load(&counter) == 1);
    pthread_barrier_destroy(&after_load);
    return 0;
}

The barrier is a test instrument. It does not corrupt the counter or weaken the atomics. It arranges an execution that was already possible for a split read/compute/write algorithm. Replacing the split sequence with atomic_fetch_add changes the algorithmic boundary and gives the result two.

This gives us a three-way classification worth retaining throughout the book:

Program Individual conflicting accesses Larger decision
Ordinary unprotected counter C data race Incorrectly decomposed increment
Atomic load plus atomic store Defined atomic accesses Still loses a logical update
Atomic fetch-add Defined atomic read-modify-write Correct increment operation

The output alone cannot tell these categories apart. The source and its synchronization argument do. This is why a later replica can be data-race-free while still returning two incompatible approvals.

Check your understanding

If A and B use two different mutexes, may both lock calls succeed? Yes. If both use the same mutex, does that choose A as the winner? No. If each increment is atomic, does a separate update to transactions become part of that atomic operation? No. These are three different questions about identity, ordering, and scope.

3. Lab: compare-and-swap and the unexpected winner

CAS stands for compare-and-swap; the C API calls it compare-and-exchange. It makes the comparison and conditional replacement one atomic operation.

For strong CAS, the conceptual rule is:

Comparison Effect on object Return Effect on expected
Object equals expected Replaced with desired Success Retains the matching expected value
Object differs Unchanged by this attempt Failure Updated with the observed object value

Let two workers try to change a slot from 0 to their own nonzero ID.

Edited source listing — cas_claim.c:

#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdatomic.h>
#include <stdio.h>

atomic_int slot = 0;

void *worker(void *arg) {
    int id = *(int *)arg;
    int expected = 0;
    if (atomic_compare_exchange_strong(&slot, &expected, id))
        printf("Thread %d CLAIMED the slot\n", id);
    else
        printf("Thread %d LOST; observed owner %d\n", id, expected);
    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t a, b;
    int id_a = 1, id_b = 2;
    pthread_create(&a, NULL, worker, &id_a);
    pthread_create(&b, NULL, worker, &id_b);
    pthread_join(a, NULL);
    pthread_join(b, NULL);
    printf("Final slot owner: %d\n", atomic_load(&slot));
    return 0;
}

Exactly one successful claim is possible in this one-shot example. Once one worker replaces 0, the other's comparison against 0 fails. The loser learns the owner observed by the failed comparison.

“How can thread 2 ever get the slot?”

Creating thread A first does not require A to reach the claim first. Thread creation order is not execution order. A may be ready while B runs; A may also run and be paused before the CAS.

The guarantee is one owner, not a predetermined owner. Scheduler nondeterminism is compatible with correctness.

The source experimented with delays to make another winner easier to see. An empty delay loop may disappear under optimization. A sleep or extra logging changes the experiment's schedule; neither is a proof of an ordering constraint unless the program uses an appropriate synchronization mechanism.

Weak and strong do not mean weaker and stronger correctness

Strong CAS does not spuriously fail when its comparison matches. Weak CAS may fail spuriously, so it is normally used where the algorithm already retries. Failure still reports an observed value through expected.

A lock-acquisition loop must restore the expected unlocked value on every attempt:

for (;;) {
    int expected = 0;
    if (atomic_compare_exchange_weak(&locked, &expected, 1))
        break;
}

If a failed attempt changes expected to 1 and the next attempt uses that value without resetting it, a 1 → 1 success could be mistaken for acquiring an already-owned lock. The surrounding protocol matters as much as the primitive.

On the x86 examples discussed, weak and strong could compile to the same lock cmpxchg instruction. On other implementations the compiler may need to hide hardware retries for strong CAS. The API contract is portable; a particular instruction mapping is an implementation detail.

The assembly connection

For an illustrative x86 cmpxchg, the accumulator supplies the expected value, another operand supplies the desired replacement, and the flags report whether the comparison succeeded. sete can turn that flag into a Boolean. A lock prefix on the memory read-modify-write supplies the relevant cross-core atomicity; it is not a pthread mutex or a command to stop every other processor.

Inspect your actual compiler's output:

gcc -std=c11 -O2 -pthread -S -masm=intel cas_claim.c -o cas_claim.s

Try: draw the two valid winning histories, then explain why both successful 0 → ID transitions cannot occur without an intervening reset.

Reading a failed CAS as useful information

Before the call, expected is a proposed premise: “I believe the slot is zero.” On success, the operation has made the conditional transition from zero to this worker's ID. On failure, the comparison supplies a different observed value. The failure is the mechanism telling the caller that its premise was not sufficient to authorize the update.

Imagine A wins with ID 1. B's call does not first pass a check and later get interrupted before replacement. The check and conditional replacement are the indivisible part. B compares against the already occupied slot, receives failure, and learns an observed owner of 1. This is why moving a plain if next to an assignment cannot reproduce CAS semantics.

The value reported through expected is still an observation made by that operation. In a general mutable system it may change again immediately thereafter. CAS does not freeze the object after returning. The slot lab is especially easy to prove because the slot is never reset: once nonzero, it stays nonzero. That monotonic rule removes many complications that will reappear in reusable locks and versioned structures.

The scheduling experiment inside the uploaded file

The supplied cas_claim.c contains a spinner() call intended to add jitter. That is a valuable experimental idea, but the particular function has several independent C problems. It is used before a matching declaration; its defined signature takes an argument while the call supplies none; and its random arithmetic can overflow signed integers. Its loop result is unused, so an optimizer may remove the intended work. Reseeding shared pseudo-random state from each worker also makes the experiment harder to interpret.

The teaching listing above omits that incidental mechanism so readers can reason about the claim itself. The submitted version is preserved in originals/. A properly specified sleep can bias arrival order without changing the single-owner rule. If one run shows worker 2 winning, that is not the atomic operation becoming less reliable. It is evidence that our assumption about which thread runs first was unjustified.

Expected value is part of the algorithm

For a one-shot ticket claim, a failure can mean “this ticket was taken; try another ticket.” For an increment loop, it can mean “recalculate the desired value from the value just observed.” For lock acquisition, it means “try again with the expected unlocked value.” Blindly reusing the failure value is correct for some algorithms and incorrect for others.

That difference is easy to miss because all three use the same function. The primitive does not know whether the caller is acquiring a lock, updating inventory, or publishing a replacement configuration. The surrounding state machine gives success and failure their meanings.

Pause and predict: if a slot can later return from 1 to 0, can two claims over the lifetime of the program succeed? Of course; they could refer to different ownership intervals. Does equality with a value prove nothing happened in between? No. That question introduces the ABA problem, which was named in the roadmap but not implemented in this progression.

One small file, three atomic operations

The uploaded big_atomic_energy.c is deliberately an assembly probe rather than an executable: it has no main. Build it as an object or assembly file. The functions illustrate fetch-add, unconditional exchange, and conditional compare-exchange. In this compact teaching copy only formatting changes.

#include <stdatomic.h>

atomic_int x = 0;

void add(void) {
    atomic_fetch_add(&x, 1);
}

void exchange(void) {
    atomic_exchange(&x, 42);
}

int cas(void) {
    int expected = 42;
    return atomic_compare_exchange_strong(&x, &expected, 100);
}

gcc -std=c11 -O2 -c big_atomic_energy.c -o big_atomic_energy.o
gcc -std=c11 -O2 -S -masm=intel big_atomic_energy.c -o big_atomic_energy.s

An unused returned old value can affect which instruction sequence a compiler chooses. Inspect the generated program rather than treating one mnemonic as the definition of the C operation. The language contract is the part on which the source-level correctness argument rests.

4. Lab: building mutual exclusion from an atomic primitive

The next realization was that atomics and mutexes are not unrelated choices. An atomic operation can establish who owns a lock; the ownership protocol then protects a much larger region of ordinary code.

C's atomic_flag_test_and_set returns the previous flag value while setting it to true. Observing false means this caller acquired an unowned flag. Observing true means it must keep waiting.

Edited source listing — spin_counter.c:

#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdatomic.h>
#include <stdio.h>

#ifndef ITERATIONS
#define ITERATIONS 1000000L
#endif

long counter = 0;
atomic_flag lock = ATOMIC_FLAG_INIT;

void spin_lock(void) {
    while (atomic_flag_test_and_set(&lock)) {
        // Busy wait
    }
}

void spin_unlock(void) {
    atomic_flag_clear(&lock);
}

void *worker(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    for (long i = 0; i < ITERATIONS; i++) {
        spin_lock();
        counter++;
        spin_unlock();
    }
    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t a, b;
    pthread_create(&a, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_create(&b, NULL, worker, NULL);
    pthread_join(a, NULL);
    pthread_join(b, NULL);
    printf("expected=%ld actual=%ld\n", 2L * ITERATIONS, counter);
    return 0;
}

The flag operations here use their default sequentially consistent ordering. They provide the ordering needed for the protected ordinary counter; a relaxed flag-only replacement would not establish the same protection for surrounding non-atomic data.

“How would the spinlock be done using a mutex?”

The spinlock is itself a lock implementation. A pthread mutex is another mechanism for establishing mutually exclusive access. You would normally replace the homemade spinlock with a mutex rather than wrap one in the other.

Mechanism What a losing participant does Scope of the guarantee
Atomic increment Completes its own increment according to atomic semantics That object operation
CAS claim Receives failure and follows the algorithm's policy Conditional object transition
Simple spinlock Actively retries lock acquisition Critical section followed by all participants
Pthread mutex May block under contention; implementation may also spin Critical section followed by all participants

Making the protected region longer can preserve the final count while wasting much more CPU on spinning. A lock owner that stops progressing can leave waiters stuck. This simple spinlock promises neither fairness nor a bounded waiting time.

The useful hierarchy: an atomic ownership transition supports a lock protocol, which protects a critical section and the invariant maintained inside it. An ordinary long is safe because of that protocol, not because its source-level increment became inherently atomic.

The flag is not protecting the counter by association

Declaring an atomic flag near an ordinary counter does not create protection. The causal argument is the actual protocol: only the caller whose atomic operation observes a clear flag may enter; all other callers remain outside; the owner clears the flag only after finishing the protected work.

Now add visibility. A handoff must do more than prevent two owners from being inside at once. The new owner must also have the ordering needed to use changes made by the previous owner. The default atomic operations in the listing supply that ordering. A more explicit conventional form uses acquire for successful test-and-set and release for clear:

while (atomic_flag_test_and_set_explicit(&lock, memory_order_acquire)) {
}
/* Protected ordinary accesses. */
atomic_flag_clear_explicit(&lock, memory_order_release);

We will unpack acquire/release in the publication chapter. Here the essential point is that reducing these to relaxed operations would change the proof for the ordinary counter, even if the flag itself remained atomic.

Busy work, blocked work, and progress

Suppose the owner spends 100 milliseconds inside the critical section. A simple spin waiter keeps executing atomic attempts during that interval. Those attempts do not help finish the owner's work. On a heavily loaded machine they may consume a CPU that some useful worker could have used. A mutex implementation may instead arrange for a waiting thread to sleep, although it may also spin briefly or have other fast paths.

This is not a categorical claim that mutexes are slow and spinlocks are fast. There are several costs: uncontended acquisition, expected waiting duration, scheduler transitions, available cores, cache traffic, and the owner's chance to run. A short critical section on an otherwise idle machine is a different workload from a lock held across a blocking operation.

A stopped lock owner also exposes a progress distinction. Other workers may keep taking steps but none can enter. An algorithm built using atomic instructions is therefore not automatically a lock-free algorithm. The progress property concerns the whole algorithm, not the name of an operation inside it. Our spinlock is a deliberately simple lock, with no fairness or owner-failure recovery guarantee.

A particularly useful comparison

The ordinary counter under the spinlock is safe for the same structural reason as the ordinary counter under the mutex: conflicting accesses are ordered by a common protocol. Its type did not become a magic exception to C's rules. If a diagnostic thread reads it without taking the lock while workers are writing, that extra participant can break the protocol.

Readers often focus only on writers. But reading an ordinary object while another thread modifies it also requires the relevant synchronization. “Read-only monitoring” is still an access to the shared state. The same lesson will matter when a database dashboard or a replica coordinator tries to inspect a state transition from outside its intended observation boundary.

5. Lab: correct programs can still fight over a cache line

The next experiment moved below C variables into physical memory behavior. Two threads can update different atomic objects correctly while contending on the same cache-coherence unit.

A cache line is a block tracked by the cache/coherence machinery. The course used an assumed 64-byte line environment. Sixty-four is a hardware assumption for this experiment, not a rule of C.

Very roughly, a coherence protocol lets multiple cores retain readable copies but coordinates ownership for conflicting writes. MESI names four states: Modified, Exclusive, Shared, Invalid. These are a useful model; real implementations include additional machinery and variations.

The important boundary mismatch is:

C algorithm sees Hardware may see
Worker A owns counter A Both counters occupy one cache line
Worker B owns counter B Both writers repeatedly need that line

This is false sharing: logically independent writes contend because of layout. Updating the exact same counter is true sharing. Neither label implies that atomic operations stop being correct.

Edited source benchmark — false_sharing.c: preserves the three cases, uses compact calls, adds result/layout checks, and defaults to fewer iterations. The original display code subtracted pointers to distinct subobjects; this listing reports standard offsetof values instead of relying on that pointer subtraction.

#define _POSIX_C_SOURCE 200809L
#include <assert.h>
#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdatomic.h>
#include <stddef.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <time.h>

#ifndef ITERATIONS
#define ITERATIONS 10000000L
#endif
#define CACHE_LINE 64

_Alignas(CACHE_LINE) atomic_long shared_counter = 0;
struct Together {
    _Alignas(CACHE_LINE) atomic_long a;
    atomic_long b;
} together;
struct Apart {
    _Alignas(CACHE_LINE) atomic_long a;
    _Alignas(CACHE_LINE) atomic_long b;
} apart;

_Static_assert(offsetof(struct Together, b) + sizeof(atomic_long) <= CACHE_LINE, "Together must fit within 64 bytes");
_Static_assert(offsetof(struct Apart, b) >= CACHE_LINE, "Apart needs separate assumed lines");

void *worker(void *arg) {
    atomic_long *counter = arg;
    for (long i = 0; i < ITERATIONS; i++)
        atomic_fetch_add_explicit(counter, 1, memory_order_relaxed);
    return NULL;
}

double now(void) {
    struct timespec ts;
    clock_gettime(CLOCK_MONOTONIC, &ts);
    return ts.tv_sec + ts.tv_nsec / 1e9;
}

void run(const char *label, atomic_long *a, atomic_long *b) {
    pthread_t ta, tb;
    atomic_store(a, 0);
    if (a != b) atomic_store(b, 0);
    double start = now();
    pthread_create(&ta, NULL, worker, a);
    pthread_create(&tb, NULL, worker, b);
    pthread_join(ta, NULL);
    pthread_join(tb, NULL);
    double elapsed = now() - start;
    long ca = atomic_load(a), cb = atomic_load(b);
    assert(ca == (a == b ? 2L * ITERATIONS : ITERATIONS));
    assert(cb == (a == b ? 2L * ITERATIONS : ITERATIONS));
    printf("%s: A=%ld B=%ld seconds=%.6f addresses=%p,%p\n", label, ca, cb, elapsed, (void *)a, (void *)b);
}

int main(void) {

    run("false sharing", &together.a, &together.b);
    run("separate lines", &apart.a, &apart.b);
    return 0;
}

_Alignas(64) requests a 64-byte boundary. The structure layout and static checks establish the intended relative placement for this build. They do not query the running processor's cache-line size.

What the experiment can establish

A large timing difference is consistent with the cache-contention mechanism, but one timing run is not a direct trace of coherence states. Core placement, shared versus separate physical cores, optimization, frequency changes, other workloads, and thread startup affect the result. Affinity or supported hardware performance counters can make a more controlled follow-up.

Putting printf in the hot loop can dominate the operation being studied. The library may synchronize internally, while I/O perturbs scheduling and cache state. Print results after the measured work when investigating the increment cost. The source's surprising per-thread progress output was a lesson about instrumentation as well as scheduling.

Why not make every coherence unit tiny?

Cache-line size is an engineering tradeoff. Tracking larger blocks can reduce metadata and exploit spatial locality; it also couples unrelated nearby writes. False sharing exposes where the nearby-access assumption fails for this workload. Padding every object has its own memory costs.

Updating adjacent b may need permission for that exact same block. The application has separate logical owners, but the hardware has a coarser ownership unit.

Why relaxed increments are sufficient here

Each worker's obligation is to perform its numeric updates atomically. There is no associated ordinary payload that another worker is supposed to start using when the count changes. We read final results after joining the workers. The program therefore does not need those increments to publish unrelated state. Relaxed describes ordering requirements, not a request for approximate arithmetic or weaker atomicity.

This is an important contrast with a ready flag. If an observer treats the flag as permission to use a separate ordinary buffer, its proof needs an ordering edge for that buffer. The same memory-order argument cannot be copied from a numeric counter simply because both variables are atomic.

A good explanation of a fast run has two parts: the counts satisfy the logical requirement, and the layout reduces a plausible performance cost. Neither statement should be used as a substitute for the other.

6. Lab: a semaphore baton and a finish barrier

The learner then asked for a stronger rule: A increments, then B, then A, then B. A mutex permits one owner at a time, but generally does not choose which waiting thread becomes the next owner.

Two semaphores can encode permission to proceed. A starts with one permit; B starts with zero. Each worker consumes its permit, touches the ordinary counter, then posts the other worker's permit.

Consolidated source listing — thread_relay.c: uses the corrected barrier API, compact formatting, and retries sem_wait when interrupted by a signal.

#define _POSIX_C_SOURCE 200809L
#include <errno.h>
#include <pthread.h>
#include <semaphore.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>

#define TURNS 10
long counter = 0;
sem_t turn_a, turn_b;
pthread_barrier_t finish_line;

void wait_turn(sem_t *turn) {
    while (sem_wait(turn) == -1) {
        if (errno == EINTR) continue;
        perror("sem_wait");
        exit(EXIT_FAILURE);
    }
}

void *worker_a(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    for (int i = 0; i < TURNS; i++) {
        wait_turn(&turn_a);
        printf("A: %ld\n", ++counter);
        sem_post(&turn_b);
    }
    puts("A reached finish barrier");
    pthread_barrier_wait(&finish_line);
    puts("A exits");
    return NULL;
}

void *worker_b(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    for (int i = 0; i < TURNS; i++) {
        wait_turn(&turn_b);
        printf("B: %ld\n", ++counter);
        sem_post(&turn_a);
    }
    puts("B reached finish barrier");
    pthread_barrier_wait(&finish_line);
    puts("B exits");
    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t a, b;
    sem_init(&turn_a, 0, 1);
    sem_init(&turn_b, 0, 0);
    pthread_barrier_init(&finish_line, NULL, 2);
    pthread_create(&a, NULL, worker_a, NULL);
    pthread_create(&b, NULL, worker_b, NULL);
    pthread_join(a, NULL);
    pthread_join(b, NULL);
    printf("Final counter: %ld\n", counter);
    sem_destroy(&turn_a);
    sem_destroy(&turn_b);
    pthread_barrier_destroy(&finish_line);
    return 0;
}

The increment lines alternate A: 1, B: 2, through B: 20. The ordinary counter is safe because the handoff orders its accesses. No atomic counter or additional mutex is necessary for this protocol.

The finish barrier establishes that neither worker proceeds past that phase until both have arrived. It does not make their next CPU instructions execute at the same instant, and it does not specify the order of their exit messages. After A posts B's last permit, B may even reach the finish barrier before A prints its own arrival message.

The compiler error was an API distinction

An earlier attempted listing used pthread_cond_wait with a barrier object. The correction was pthread_barrier_wait(&finish_line). A condition variable is a different mechanism; its wait operation also needs an associated mutex. The mismatch did not indicate a flaw in semaphore handoff.

Primitive Rule expressed
Atomic operation This particular object transition is indivisible
Mutex One participating owner may execute the protected region
Semaphore A count of permits/events controls progress
Two-semaphore baton Permission is handed to a particular next participant
Barrier A phase cannot finish until all required participants arrive

These mechanisms constrain legal histories. They do not prescribe a complete scheduler timeline. If a participant never reaches a required barrier, the others can wait indefinitely; correct participant counts and reachable phases are part of the assumptions.

Try: explain why replacing the baton with an ordinary shared counter and two unconstrained loops loses the alternation guarantee even if the counter itself becomes atomic.

Trace the permits rather than the CPU scheduler

Initially A has permission and B does not. Suppose the scheduler runs B first. B reaches sem_wait and waits; it does not increment first merely because it received CPU time first. When A eventually runs, it consumes its permit, increments, and posts B's semaphore. B can then continue.

After A posts B, A may run again immediately. But A's next attempt to consume its own semaphore cannot succeed until B posts it. This is the stronger rule that a single mutex would not provide. A mutex prevents overlapping critical sections but may allow A to acquire repeatedly. The two-semaphore protocol encodes which participant becomes eligible next.

Moment A permit B permit Who can next increment?
Initial 1 0 A
A consumes permit 0 0 A is already inside its turn
A finishes and posts B 0 1 B
B consumes and later posts A 1 0 A

The table describes the protocol, not necessarily values that can be sampled simultaneously from outside it. It is a reasoning model for how permission moves.

The ordinary counter has an ordered ownership chain

The critical fact is not that the variable is at one stable address. That was the confusion that later made the ready-flag example look contradictory. A stable address can still be read and written incorrectly. The semaphore handoff supplies the synchronization that makes the ordinary accesses valid. The storage location tells us which object is being accessed; the handoff establishes when access is permitted.

The last turn also explains the barrier's role. A posts B's final permit before A reaches the finish barrier. B can therefore finish and arrive at the barrier first. Both arrival messages must precede both exit messages, but there is no required ordering between the two arrivals or the two exits. “Both released” is a statement about eligibility, not simultaneous physical execution.

Semaphore permits and condition-variable notifications differ

A semaphore carries a count. Posting before a later wait makes a permit available. A condition variable is not a persistent queue of permission tokens. Its normal pattern is a shared predicate under a mutex, a loop that checks the predicate, and a wait that atomically releases the mutex while blocking, then reacquires it before returning.

A notification asks a waiter to check the predicate again. It does not prove that the predicate is now true, nor does a notification sent with no waiter become a future permit. This distinction will explain a failed scheduling experiment in the replica chapter. We will not try to reproduce semaphore behavior by scattering an unprotected Boolean and cond_signal through unrelated critical sections.

Predict first: if both semaphores begin at zero, what makes the first increment possible? Nothing. If both begin at one, what forbids both workers from touching the ordinary counter together? The required baton invariant has disappeared. Initialization is part of the synchronization algorithm.

7. Why memory and isolation have several different boundaries

The cache example prompted a broader question: if C code can reach ordinary memory so directly, what makes process, container, and VM boundaries real?

The answer requires distinguishing the units that different mechanisms track.

Layer or mechanism Relevant unit or responsibility
C object model Object lifetime, type, bounds, and valid operations
Allocator Allocated/free chunks and allocator metadata
Cache coherence Cache lines and cross-core visibility/ownership
Virtual memory Page mappings and access permissions
Kernel Processes, credentials, resources, namespaces, and policy
Virtualization Guest execution and guest-to-host memory/resource boundaries

malloc metadata does not normally cause the processor to check an object's allocation bounds on every pointer dereference. An invalid C access can be undefined behavior while landing in a mapped page that produces no immediate hardware fault. A crash in free may expose corruption that occurred earlier.

Page protection is different from allocator bookkeeping. Likewise, coherence maintains the required consistency of accesses; it does not decide whether application code was authorized to issue a pointer access.

Virtual addresses are not physical identity

Two processes can use the same numerical virtual address while referring to different physical memory. Merely matching virtual addresses does not create shared data or cache-line contention on that data. Conversely, explicitly shared mappings can expose the same physical storage through different virtual addresses.

Threads in a process share its address space. An application's intended per-thread ownership of two adjacent fields is not a hardware protection boundary, which is why false sharing is possible in the earlier lab.

Containers and VMs change the enforcement assumptions

The discussion connected this to the trusted computing base: components whose correct behavior a guarantee depends on.

Containers commonly rely on a shared host kernel to maintain isolation through permissions, namespaces, resource controls, and related mechanisms. A VM adds a guest boundary, including virtualization-controlled translation between guest physical and host physical memory on the model discussed.

Compromising a guest kernel does not, by itself, grant control of the hypervisor's mappings. Nor does a VM provide a literal physical wall: virtualization code, device interfaces, and lower-level hardware remain part of the relevant assumptions.

The conceptual comparison from the supplied video discussion is retained; this edition does not reproduce or independently evaluate the linked video's product recommendations. The lesson is the enforcement question:

Which lower-level mechanism makes the claimed higher-level boundary real?

A performance symptom, a memory-safety violation, and an isolation escape are different outcomes. A common underlying habit is to identify the actual boundary rather than infer one from a convenient software label.

The same byte participates in several different systems

Imagine an allocation holding two fields. The allocator may regard it as one live chunk. C regards each field as an object with language rules about valid access. The page tables make the page readable or writable. The cache tracks a line containing some of those bytes. These mechanisms are not competing descriptions; they answer different questions about the same storage.

This is why an out-of-bounds write can evade an immediate hardware fault. The write may land on a mapped writable page while violating the language's object bounds and damaging another allocation. The MMU is not normally consulting malloc's allocation metadata to discover that the pointer crossed an application object boundary. The mistake remains a C defect even if the processor completes the access.

The converse is equally useful. Correct C logic operating through an invalid mapping can fault. A correct language-level access protocol does not grant permission to read arbitrary physical memory. The layers' assumptions have to fit together.

A shared cache is not the same as shared writable address space

The source's question about unrelated programs “touching the same 64 bytes” needed one refinement. Matching virtual addresses in separate processes can map to different physical pages. The numerical pointer alone does not identify a shared physical cache line. Shared mappings, by contrast, can create genuine sharing even when the processes use different virtual addresses.

Also distinguish direct access from indirect interference. Two workloads can compete for a shared hardware resource without either receiving permission to dereference the other's memory. Performance interference and side-channel questions concern what can be inferred from shared resources; an ordinary unauthorized direct read is a different mechanism. Neither should be waved away merely because a process abstraction exists.

Why the container and VM connection mattered

The original discussion became more coherent once “container” and “VM” were replaced by the mechanism doing the enforcement. A container boundary commonly depends on the host kernel enforcing a collection of policies. A guest VM also has a kernel, but its view of physical memory is itself constrained by a lower virtualization boundary.

That does not mean a guest kernel is unimportant or the lower layer is infallible. It means a particular guest compromise does not automatically include authority over the hypervisor's mappings. The next questions are which interfaces cross that boundary and which components must be trusted for the intended isolation claim.

This is the same question we asked about the counter: who is participating in the protection protocol, and what can bypass it? The mechanism is vastly different, but the form of reasoning is familiar. We are no longer satisfied with “the bytes belong to this object” or “the process is inside a container” as a complete causal explanation of safety.

9. Lab: publishing ordinary data with acquire/release

The baton already proved an ordering. The next question was whether an atomic ready flag can publish an ordinary payload without a semaphore or mutex.

An atomic flag with only relaxed accesses makes accesses to that flag well-defined. It does not establish the required cross-thread ordering for an ordinary int data:

/* Deliberately broken publication pattern: data is non-atomic. */
data = 42;
atomic_store_explicit(&ready, 1, memory_order_relaxed);

/* Another thread: no synchronizes-with edge for data. */
while (atomic_load_explicit(&ready, memory_order_relaxed) == 0) {
}
printf("%d\n", data);

This pattern can have a C data race on data. It is not a portable experiment that promises merely an occasional old value, even if it repeatedly prints 42 on the machine tested.

Edited source listing — publication.c: the corrected one-shot version. It was introduced and explained in the original thread; this edition's new validation run is separate evidence, not a claim that the learner demonstrated both outcomes there.

#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdatomic.h>
#include <stdio.h>

int data = 0;
atomic_int ready = 0;

void *producer(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    data = 42;
    atomic_store_explicit(&ready, 1, memory_order_release);
    return NULL;
}

void *consumer(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    while (atomic_load_explicit(&ready, memory_order_acquire) == 0) {
    }
    printf("data = %d\n", data);
    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t a, b;
    pthread_create(&a, NULL, producer, NULL);
    pthread_create(&b, NULL, consumer, NULL);
    pthread_join(a, NULL);
    pthread_join(b, NULL);
    return 0;
}

When the acquire load observes the release store's value, that store synchronizes with the load. The ordinary payload write before the store therefore happens-before the payload read after the load.

Event Reason for its order
Producer writes data Sequenced before publishing ready
Producer release-stores 1 Publication event
Consumer acquire-load observes that 1 Synchronizes with the release publication
Consumer reads data Sequenced after the observing load

The data can remain an ordinary integer because its conflicting accesses are now ordered. The payload is not modified again in this one-shot program. Repeated publication needs additional rules so a writer cannot overwrite a payload while a reader still uses it; the one-shot example does not solve that broader problem.

Memory ordering is not memory layout

The question that clarified this was why the baton counter could be a plain long, while the new ready flag needed an atomic type.

The baton has synchronization around the counter's accesses. The ready flag is itself part of the synchronization mechanism and is directly polled by one thread while another updates it. The acquire/release flag then orders access to a different, ordinary payload.

Concept Concern
Layout Where bytes and objects are placed; alignment and cache-line sharing
Ordering Which relationships between operations the language model establishes
Lifetime Whether the object still exists and access remains valid

Relaxed atomics are useful when only that atomic object's value matters, as in a numeric count without a payload-publication requirement. The default sequentially consistent operations used in earlier labs provide stronger ordering, but still do not make several distinct operations one transaction.

The source also noted that acquire/release and relaxed loads or stores can compile to similar instructions on x86. Identical-looking instructions do not erase the C-level compiler constraints. Inspect actual output; do not infer portable guarantees from one architecture's behavior.

Follow the exact permission carried by ready

The producer first writes the payload and then release-stores 1 into ready. The consumer is allowed to use the payload only after an acquire load observes that publication. Merely executing an acquire operation somewhere in the program does not import every write ever made by every thread. The observation connecting these two operations is essential.

In this one-shot program, zero means “do not use the payload yet,” and one means the publication has occurred. The producer does not modify the payload after publishing it. The consumer does not read it speculatively and keep that read if the flag later changes. These small state-machine facts are what make the example easy to prove.

Reader, condensed: Why was an ordinary long safe in the relay, but now the flag needs to be atomic? Did changing its memory location matter?

Explanation: The relay has semaphores supplying the synchronization. The flag has no enclosing semaphore or mutex: one thread polls it while another updates it, so its accesses need their own defined coordination. That atomic flag can then supply an ordering edge for an ordinary payload. Layout did not answer either question.

A sentence that should be used carefully

“Acquire makes the data visible” is useful shorthand, but can conceal the proof. The fuller statement is: the producer's payload write is sequenced before its release; the consumer's acquire observes that release; the consumer's subsequent payload read is therefore ordered after the producer's write. That is the relationship the C model provides.

No physical stopwatch measurement substitutes for that relationship. A producer may appear to finish before a consumer starts using a buffer in several tests, while the program lacks the synchronization required by the language. Similarly, a relaxed flag may appear to work on a particular compiler and CPU without establishing a portable contract for the ordinary payload.

Why the repeated-use version is a different problem

Suppose the producer publishes 42, then immediately writes 43 into the same ordinary payload for the next message. The consumer might still be reading the first publication. One acquire observation does not grant exclusive ownership of a perpetually reused buffer.

A reusable design needs another rule: an acknowledgement, a ring-buffer ownership protocol, a lock, independent immutable versions, or some other mechanism that prevents overwriting a payload still in use. Those are design alternatives to investigate, not consequences of changing a memory-order keyword in the one-shot example.

Check: would making only data atomic solve the publication interpretation? It would define that object's individual accesses, but the question of which message/version the consumer is accepting would still need a protocol. Atomic types can remove one class of undefined behavior without completing the algorithm.

    for (int i = 0; i < THREADS; i++)
        pthread_join(threads[i], NULL);

    printf("Remaining: %d\n", atomic_load(&remaining));
    return 0;
}

At the last ticket, A and B can both load 1. A decrements to 0; B then atomically decrements to -1. No individual atomic operation was violated. The combined check-and-change was not protected.

A particular run can finish at 0 because it did not encounter that schedule. This source program does not force the rare window. The later one-seat lab provides a deliberately arranged, well-defined logical failure.

Version B: protect the whole decision with a mutex

Edited source listing — inventory_mutex.c:

#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdio.h>

#define THREADS 8
#define TICKETS 100
#define ATTEMPTS 100000

int remaining = TICKETS;
pthread_mutex_t lock = PTHREAD_MUTEX_INITIALIZER;

void *worker(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    for (int i = 0; i < ATTEMPTS; i++) {
        pthread_mutex_lock(&lock);

        if (remaining > 0)
            remaining--;

        pthread_mutex_unlock(&lock);
    }

    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t threads[THREADS];

    for (int i = 0; i < THREADS; i++)
        pthread_create(&threads[i], NULL, worker, NULL);

    for (int i = 0; i < THREADS; i++)
        pthread_join(threads[i], NULL);

    printf("Remaining: %d\n", remaining);
    return 0;
}

The ordinary integer is sufficient because both the availability check and its decrement occur under one shared mutex. Main reads it after joining all workers. With successful thread execution and enough attempts, the final inventory is zero and never negative.

Version C: validate and decrement with CAS

Edited source listing — inventory_cas.c:

#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdatomic.h>
#include <stdio.h>

#define THREADS 8
#define TICKETS 100
#define ATTEMPTS 100000

atomic_int remaining = TICKETS;

int try_buy_ticket(void) {
    int current = atomic_load_explicit(&remaining, memory_order_relaxed);

    while (current > 0) {
        if (atomic_compare_exchange_weak_explicit(&remaining, &current, current - 1, memory_order_relaxed, memory_order_relaxed)) {
            return 1;
        }
    }

    return 0;
}

void *worker(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    for (int i = 0; i < ATTEMPTS; i++)
        try_buy_ticket();

    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t threads[THREADS];

    for (int i = 0; i < THREADS; i++)
        pthread_create(&threads[i], NULL, worker, NULL);

    for (int i = 0; i < THREADS; i++)
        pthread_join(threads[i], NULL);

    printf("Remaining: %d\n", atomic_load(&remaining));
    return 0;
}

A successful CAS commits current → current - 1 only if that positive current still matches. On failure, current is refreshed with the observed value, and the loop reevaluates availability. Spurious weak-CAS failures are also retried. The complete program still relies on progress of participating threads; it does not promise a bounded number of retries.

The bridge into the continuation

The original source proposed adding sold and requiring an accounting relationship such as remaining + sold == TICKETS. This needs an explicit observation boundary. Even under a mutex, the two machine-level assignments occur separately; cooperating readers taking the same mutex can observe the complete logical operation. Independent unlocked reads do not inherit that transactional view.

The continuation revisited these same issues through immutable publication, database updates, a one-seat reservation, and multi-field snapshots. We now have the foundation needed to follow that progression without treating each new synchronization API as an isolated trick.

Draw the permission gap at the last ticket

The critical input is not how many workers exist overall. It is that at least two can make a decision from the same positive observation.

Event Inventory A's local observation B's local observation
A loads 1 1 —
B loads 1 1 1
A decrements 0 1 1
B decrements -1 1 1

Both decrements are perfectly atomic. The algorithm lacks an operation that means “decrement only while the inventory still satisfies the decision's premise.” Sequentially consistent loads and decrements would still permit this trace. Blaming memory_order_relaxed would misidentify the problem.

A mutex puts both the current-value check and decrement inside one region. The CAS loop instead proposes a transition based on the observed count and commits only if that count still matches. When it fails, it revisits the premise using the value reported by the failed comparison.

What does a retry actually repeat?

If the first observed value is 10, a worker proposes 9. If another worker wins first, the local current may become 9. The next attempt proposes 8. It must not keep trying to store the old desired value 9 after changing its expected value to 9, because that would no longer describe buying a ticket. The desired value is derived again from the current premise.

If failure updates current to zero, the loop exits without issuing a successful reservation. If a weak comparison fails spuriously while the value remains positive, the loop can try again. There is no need to interpret every failed attempt as another customer sale or a corrupt state.

Tempting repairs and the property they actually offer

One possible idea is to decrement unconditionally, inspect the returned old value, and add one back if there was no stock. Such a design can sometimes be made to count successful claims under a carefully chosen contract. It does not automatically preserve “the shared count is never negative at any observable point.” Other code could inspect the negative intermediate value, perform another action, or make a decision before compensation occurs.

That is why the property must precede the proposed repair. A final corrected number is a different promise from preventing invalid intermediate states from becoming observable to other participants. We need not reject every compensating design; we must state the actual semantics it implements.

Exercise: replace “tickets” with “coupon not yet redeemed.” What must be inside the guarded transition? The test for redemption and the change that claims it. A separate later email or charge also needs its own relationship to the successful claim, which a local CAS does not establish by itself.

11. Immutability moves coordination to publication

Suppose many threads read a configuration with two related fields:

struct Config {
    int max_connections;
    int timeout_ms;
};

A mutable design can protect reads and updates with a common mutex. A writer updates both fields while holding the lock; readers take that same lock while copying the fields they need. This works when the entire relevant operation is inside the agreed boundary.

An alternative is to construct a new version privately, then publish one reference to it:

_Atomic(struct Config *) current;

/* Writer: initialize every field of next before publishing it. */
atomic_store_explicit(&current, next, memory_order_release);

/* Reader: load once, then use that one immutable version. */
struct Config *cfg = atomic_load_explicit(&current, memory_order_acquire);
int limit = cfg->max_connections;
int timeout = cfg->timeout_ms;

This is a pattern fragment, not a complete program: initialization, allocation, and reclamation are additional responsibilities. An acquire load that observes the release publication makes the preceding initialization available to the reader. The published object must then remain immutable.

The question that clarified the comparison

Does the globally scoped mutex approach fail?

No. Both designs can be correct. A global mutex works only when every relevant reader and writer uses it and holds it for the necessary scope. Immutable publication changes where the coordination cost occurs.

Design Reader work Writer work Remaining challenge
Mutable object under a mutex Acquire lock and copy/read state Update inside the lock Contention and critical-section scope
Immutable versions Load a reference and read one version Build and publish a new version Publication, conflicting writers, and object lifetime

Read-heavy workloads can benefit from avoiding a lock operation on every read. Copying large objects for frequent updates can instead be expensive. These are workload questions, not a ranking of primitives.

Two problems publication does not solve

First, the old version cannot be freed while a reader still uses it. A writer's replacement of current does not revoke existing readers' references. Reference counting, epochs, hazard pointers, and RCU were named as related approaches, but their implementation was not completed here.

Second, two writers may both derive replacements from the same old version. If both use unconditional stores, one writer's changes can disappear. When updates depend on prior state, the publication step may need a writer lock or compare-and-swap with recomputation after conflict. An atomic reference store makes replacement indivisible; it does not merge independent changes.

Takeaway: immutable objects simplify shared reading, while coordination remains at publication and reclamation boundaries.

One pointer can select a large coherent object

The advantage of immutable publication is not that the new configuration has only one field. It is that one chosen reference identifies a version whose fields will not change under the reader. The reader loads the reference once and uses that same version for the operation.

If it loads current separately for each field, it can accidentally select one field from version A and another from version B. The underlying versions are individually immutable, but the reader has assembled a result spanning two versions. “The objects never change” does not excuse a poorly defined snapshot boundary.

This is another granularity choice. What belongs in one version? If timeout_ms and max_connections must be interpreted together, selecting one shared configuration object gives that relationship a clear boundary. If the entire application is copied for every tiny change, copying may be too expensive. Structural sharing or smaller versioned components can reduce copying, but relationships between components then need explicit treatment.

The difficult work has moved, not disappeared

Reader, condensed: So the atomic publication still needs ownership to change when two writers publish. Is the benefit simply that the conflict is much smaller?

Explanation: Yes. Readers mostly perform shared loads of immutable storage, while writers contend over selecting the current version. But two writers deriving changes from the same old version still need to preserve their intended update semantics.

Step Writer 1 Writer 2
Read Selects A Selects A
Work privately Builds B with one change Builds C with another change
Publish Stores B Stores C

Unconditional pointer stores permit C to replace B, losing writer 1's change if both changes were supposed to accumulate. If the contract is “replace the whole configuration with this independent value,” that may be acceptable. If the contract is “apply my change to the current configuration,” the writer needs conflict validation or serialization.

A pointer CAS can validate the assumed base version. On failure, rebuilding from the newly observed version may preserve the other writer's change. However, a full implementation also needs to keep referenced versions alive while writers and readers use them. Do not free a failed or retired base object simply because one CAS returned; other participants may hold it.

const is a discipline, not a reclamation system

A const-qualified access path helps express an intention not to mutate through that path. It does not provide lifetime management or stop every other alias from making an otherwise invalid modification. The safe design includes the publication protocol, the promise that readers see immutable versions, and a reclamation rule.

This is why immutability connected so strongly to the camera case: both require proof that every relevant participant has finished before storage is reclaimed. The publication point can be tiny even when the selected state is large; the lifetime obligation still spans all participants using it.

12. Database transactions and stale decisions

The same shape appears when application code reads a database value, calculates outside the database, and later writes a replacement.

Start with a balance of 100. Two clients read it. One computes a withdrawal of 20 and plans to write 80. The other computes a deposit of 50 and plans to write 150.

Step Client A Client B
Observe Reads 100 Reads 100
Calculate privately Computes 80 Computes 150
Update Writes literal 80 Writes literal 150

Serializing the final writes alone still permits a final balance of 80 or 150, depending on order. Neither represents applying both intended changes; that result would be 130.

A lock on the final assignment does not protect an earlier assumption. This is time-of-check/time-of-use reasoning: the state was valid when observed, but may have changed before the decision commits.

Express the operation inside its protection boundary

For the single-row arithmetic example discussed in the thread, putting the operation in the database allows it to use the relevant current row:

UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - 20 WHERE id = 1;
UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance + 50 WHERE id = 1;

Conditional updates can express the invariant directly:

UPDATE inventory
SET quantity = quantity - 1
WHERE product_id = 42 AND quantity > 0;

The caller checks whether the update affected a row. A prior successful read is not evidence that this later conditional update succeeded.

Two other patterns discussed were holding a row lock across a transaction's read/decision/write sequence, and validating a version:

UPDATE accounts
SET balance = 80, version = version + 1
WHERE id = 1 AND version = 42;

Zero affected rows means the expected version was not matched. The application must handle that outcome, commonly by rereading and retrying. These SQL fragments illustrate the conversation's single-row examples; complete transaction design must account for its actual isolation semantics and every row or predicate involved.

Versions do not automatically reconcile writers

MVCC gives readers access to appropriate row versions. That does not mean two writers can independently modify the same row and have their business intent merged afterward. Writer conflict handling is a separate requirement. Similarly, serializable execution means committed behavior corresponds to a valid serial history; it does not require literally executing all work one transaction at a time.

The thread contrasted versioned readers with a single-owner execution model: multiple clients can submit work while one executor serializes mutations. Both approaches relocate coordination. Neither removes the need to define valid transitions.

Provisional results and committed results

The discussion also connected this to the Polymarket “Ghost Fills” example: one component can report a provisional outcome before another component has committed the underlying transition. The general lesson is to preserve the distinction between observed, validated at an earlier time, and committed. This course uses that architectural connection without reproducing or independently verifying the external incident claims.

Takeaway: protect the relationship between observation, decision, and commit, rather than only the final write.

The database did not misunderstand the two assignments

If the application asks the database to set the balance to 80 and later to 150, serializing those assignments can correctly produce 150. The database has not been told that 80 meant “subtract 20 from whichever balance is current” or that 150 meant “add 50.” That business intent existed only in the clients' earlier private calculations.

This was the exact point of surprise in the discussion: a storage system can protect its internal data structures and execute each requested update correctly while the application's overall operation loses an intended change. The missing information is the dependency of each replacement on the old balance.

Follow both possible orders

For operation expressions, the simple single-row example has two histories:

Order First operation Second operation Final balance
Withdrawal then deposit 100 − 20 = 80 80 + 50 = 130 130
Deposit then withdrawal 100 + 50 = 150 150 − 20 = 130 130

For stale literal replacements, the histories are different:

Order First replacement Second replacement Final balance
A then B Set 80 Set 150 150
B then A Set 150 Set 80 80

The arithmetic happens to commute in the first example. Other operations may not commute, so “both orders have the same answer” is not the general definition of correctness. The general requirement is the ordering and transactional behavior promised for the operations being submitted.

PostgreSQL's documented Read Committed behavior for a conflicting update to a predetermined row includes waiting for the first updater and, if it commits, checking the updated row before applying the later operation. This supports the simple example. It is not a promise that every multi-row application invariant is automatically preserved at that isolation level.

Express a condition where it can be checked at commit

A version predicate makes the dependency explicit: “this replacement was computed from version 42; only apply it if that version still matches.” Checking the affected-row count is part of that protocol. Ignoring a zero-row result and telling the customer the operation succeeded would undo the benefit of the validation mechanism at the application level.

Alternatively, a transaction can lock the relevant row before reading and keep the protection through its decision and write. The useful interval starts with the observation on which the decision depends, not merely the last assignment. More involved predicates or multiple rows require a transaction design appropriate to those relationships.

Reader, condensed: So a concurrency-safe database cannot make arbitrary application code safe automatically?

Explanation: Correct. It provides operations with defined semantics. The application must express its intended invariant using those operations, constraints, and transaction boundaries. That is the same lesson as the private mutex and early unlock, at a different abstraction layer.

13. Lab: one seat, two buyers

There is one seat. Two buyers attempt to reserve it. At the end:

  • At most one ticket may have been issued.
  • Successful sales and remaining seats must account for the original seat.

The source's first unprotected C program used ordinary shared integers. It therefore had C data races as well as the intended stale-decision bug. Its output cannot be promised under the C language rules.

The edited lab below uses atomics in all three modes to isolate the logical bug. In naive mode, both buyers read 1 before a barrier releases them. Both then store 0 and issue a ticket. Atomic reads and stores make these accesses well-defined without making the whole reservation correct.

Edited teaching version — seats.c:

#define _POSIX_C_SOURCE 200809L
#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdatomic.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <string.h>

enum { NAIVE, MUTEX, CAS };
atomic_int seats = 1, sold = 0;
pthread_mutex_t lock = PTHREAD_MUTEX_INITIALIZER;
pthread_barrier_t gate;
int mode;

void *reserve(void *arg) {
    const char *name = arg;
    if (mode == MUTEX) {
        pthread_barrier_wait(&gate); // Rendezvous before taking the lock
        pthread_mutex_lock(&lock);
        if (atomic_load(&seats) > 0) {
            atomic_fetch_sub(&seats, 1);
            atomic_fetch_add(&sold, 1);
            printf("%s bought a ticket\n", name);
        }
        pthread_mutex_unlock(&lock);
    } else {
        int expected = atomic_load(&seats);
        pthread_barrier_wait(&gate); // Both observed the original seat count
        if (expected > 0) {
            if (mode == NAIVE) {
                atomic_store(&seats, expected - 1);
                atomic_fetch_add(&sold, 1);
                printf("%s bought a ticket from a stale snapshot\n", name);
            } else if (atomic_compare_exchange_strong(&seats, &expected, expected - 1)) {
                atomic_fetch_add(&sold, 1);
                printf("%s bought the ticket\n", name);
            } else {
                printf("%s lost the race; seats now %d\n", name, expected);
            }
        }
    }
    return NULL;
}

int main(int argc, char **argv) {
    if (argc != 2 || (strcmp(argv[1], "naive") && strcmp(argv[1], "mutex") && strcmp(argv[1], "cas"))) {
        fprintf(stderr, "usage: %s naive|mutex|cas\n", argv[0]);
        return 2;
    }
    mode = !strcmp(argv[1], "naive") ? NAIVE : !strcmp(argv[1], "mutex") ? MUTEX : CAS;
    pthread_t a, b;
    pthread_barrier_init(&gate, NULL, 2);
    pthread_create(&a, NULL, reserve, "A");
    pthread_create(&b, NULL, reserve, "B");
    pthread_join(a, NULL);
    pthread_join(b, NULL);
    printf("remaining=%d sold=%d\n", atomic_load(&seats), atomic_load(&sold));
    pthread_barrier_destroy(&gate);
    pthread_mutex_destroy(&lock);
    return 0;
}

Run each mode:

./seats naive
./seats mutex
./seats cas
Mode Final remaining seats Final tickets sold Mechanism
Naive 0 2 Both commit from the same stale snapshot
Mutex 0 1 Check and change share one critical section
CAS 0 1 Only one expected-value comparison can claim the seat

These totals were reproduced in the new validation run. Buyer names and print order may differ.

Why both correct versions work

The mutex version prevents another buyer from intervening between checking availability and recording the sale. The CAS version permits simultaneous reasoning but conditions the claim on the state still matching the expected value. On CAS failure, C updates expected with the value observed by the comparison.

This example uses strong CAS so failure does not include a spurious failure with an unchanged value. A weak-CAS loop must also account for that possibility.

A barrier is a rendezvous, not a reservation rule

The barrier makes an interesting schedule easy to observe. It does not decide who owns the seat. In the mutex branch it is before lock acquisition. In the other branches it is after both snapshots have been read.

Putting a two-thread barrier inside a mutex that both participants must acquire can deadlock: one thread holds the mutex and waits for the other, while the other cannot reach the barrier because it needs that mutex.

A boundary worth noticing

The successful CAS reserves the seat; the sale counter is incremented in a separate operation. After both threads finish, the accounting is correct. An arbitrary observer reading the two counters independently does not get a transactional snapshot. The invariant's observation boundary matters.

Try: explain the exact instant at which a reservation becomes committed in each correct mode. Then explain why a plausible final seats == 0 does not establish that the naive version sold only one ticket.

Why the two correct reservation programs looked so similar

The learner expected “optimistic” to allow something resembling the first unprotected program. The name seemed to suggest accepting a risk of incoherence for speed. The experiment corrected that interpretation.

Both buyers may read the same available seat. The optimistic design is prepared for that. What it refuses to do is let both callers turn that observation into a successful claim. The validation gate is the essential part of optimism, not an optional cleanup stage.

The pessimistic design pays by excluding overlap in the protected decision. The optimistic design permits overlap in the reasoning, then one attempted commit fails. Neither is permitted to issue a second valid ticket for the same seat under this contract. The possible customer-visible outcome is still one success and one failure; the mechanism and wasted work differ.

Follow the exact contents of the local variables

A and B both load expected = 1. Each computes desired = 0. Suppose A's strong CAS succeeds. B's later comparison sees zero instead of one, leaves the seat at zero, and overwrites B's expected with that observed zero. B's printed failure is positive evidence that the conflicting claim was rejected. It is not evidence that the system briefly issued a bad ticket.

In the mutex version, B might never get to reason from a seat count of one inside the lock: it acquires after A and sees zero immediately. That avoids this particular speculative failure by making B wait earlier. Waiting is the cost that does not appear in the optimistic “lost races” counter.

The plain original and the controlled teaching version

The uploaded reservations.c forces both snapshots before the writes, but uses ordinary shared integers for the conflicting writes. It has a C data race in addition to the logical overselling structure. Treat its plausible printed result as a symptom, not a portable deterministic contract.

The combined teaching seats.c uses atomic accesses even in its deliberately wrong mode. That lets the book promise and check the logical failure without asking undefined C behavior to serve as a reliable experiment. This edit preserves the lesson while making its observation much more precise.

The uploaded pessimistic example initializes a barrier it does not use. That barrier contributes no protection. The mutex is sufficient for the check-and-update operation. The optimistic example uses its barrier to expose the common snapshot; CAS is the mechanism preserving the single-claim rule. Removing a test barrier changes how often a failure attempt occurs, not the core correctness argument.

Check: are “customer B did not obtain this seat” and “a bad ticket was issued to B” interchangeable? No. A failed acquisition can be exactly the correct response to contention.

14. Lab: many uniquely numbered tickets

One seat hides much of the performance distinction because every buyer competes for the same resource. Giving tickets independent IDs allows us to ask whether unrelated reservations need to coordinate.

The source compared a global table mutex with one atomic owner per ticket. Ticket IDs are array indices. Owner -1 means free; other values identify the worker that claimed the ticket.

Edited source program — ticket_compare.c: the algorithm is preserved; post-run ownership/count assertions have been added, and the CAS call has been compacted.

#define _POSIX_C_SOURCE 200809L

#include <assert.h>
#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdatomic.h>
#include <stdint.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <time.h>

#define TICKETS 100000
#define THREADS 8

enum { PESSIMISTIC, OPTIMISTIC };

_Atomic int owner[TICKETS];
_Atomic int sold = 0;

pthread_mutex_t lock = PTHREAD_MUTEX_INITIALIZER;

struct Stats {
    int id;
    int mode;
    long attempts;
    long occupied;
    long races;
    long wins;
};

uint32_t rng(uint32_t *state) {
    *state = *state * 1664525u + 1013904223u;
    return *state;
}

void *worker(void *arg) {
    struct Stats *s = arg;
    uint32_t seed = 0x9e3779b9u ^ (s->id + 1);

    while (atomic_load_explicit(&sold, memory_order_relaxed) < TICKETS) {
        int ticket = rng(&seed) % TICKETS;
        s->attempts++;

        if (s->mode == PESSIMISTIC) {
            pthread_mutex_lock(&lock);

            if (atomic_load_explicit(&owner[ticket], memory_order_relaxed) == -1) {
                atomic_store_explicit(&owner[ticket], s->id, memory_order_relaxed);
                atomic_fetch_add_explicit(&sold, 1, memory_order_relaxed);
                s->wins++;
            } else {
                s->occupied++;
            }

            pthread_mutex_unlock(&lock);
        } else {
            if (atomic_load_explicit(&owner[ticket], memory_order_relaxed) != -1) {
                s->occupied++;
                continue;
            }

            int expected = -1;

            if (atomic_compare_exchange_strong_explicit(&owner[ticket], &expected, s->id, memory_order_relaxed, memory_order_relaxed)) {

                atomic_fetch_add_explicit(&sold, 1, memory_order_relaxed);
                s->wins++;
            } else {
                s->races++;
            }
        }
    }

    return NULL;
}

double now(void) {
    struct timespec ts;
    clock_gettime(CLOCK_MONOTONIC, &ts);
    return ts.tv_sec + ts.tv_nsec / 1e9;
}

void run_test(int mode) {
    pthread_t threads[THREADS];
    struct Stats stats[THREADS];

    atomic_store(&sold, 0);

    for (int i = 0; i < TICKETS; i++)
        atomic_store(&owner[i], -1);

    double start = now();

    for (int i = 0; i < THREADS; i++) {
        stats[i] = (struct Stats){
            .id = i,
            .mode = mode
        };

        pthread_create(&threads[i], NULL, worker, &stats[i]);
    }

    for (int i = 0; i < THREADS; i++)
        pthread_join(threads[i], NULL);

    double end = now();
    int claimed = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < TICKETS; i++) {
        int id = atomic_load(&owner[i]);
        assert(id >= 0 && id < THREADS);
        claimed++;
    }
    assert(claimed == TICKETS && atomic_load(&sold) == TICKETS);

    long attempts = 0;
    long occupied = 0;
    long races = 0;
    long wins = 0;

    for (int i = 0; i < THREADS; i++) {
        attempts += stats[i].attempts;
        occupied += stats[i].occupied;
        races += stats[i].races;
        wins += stats[i].wins;
    }

    assert(wins == TICKETS);
    printf("\n%s\n", mode == PESSIMISTIC ? "PESSIMISTIC" : "OPTIMISTIC");
    printf("tickets sold: %ld\n", wins);
    printf("attempts:     %ld\n", attempts);
    printf("occupied:     %ld\n", occupied);
    printf("lost races:   %ld\n", races);
    printf("time:         %.6f s\n", end - start);
}

int main(void) {
    run_test(PESSIMISTIC);
    run_test(OPTIMISTIC);
}

What the counters measure

Counter Meaning
wins Successful claims by this worker
occupied Chosen ticket was already owned when checked
races Ticket looked free, but the later strong CAS did not acquire it
attempts Total randomly chosen ticket IDs examined

The crucial clarification was: a lost race is a failed attempt to acquire a valid ticket, not an invalid ticket being issued. Both implementations must preserve the ownership rule. Optimism concerns whether speculative work will survive validation, not whether correctness is optional.

The pessimistic version prevents a competing claim within the protected operation. The optimistic version allows the overlap, then rejects a stale claim. Both can be correct while paying different coordination costs.

How to read the benchmark honestly

This experiment compares more than mutex instructions against CAS instructions. It also compares one global lock with per-ticket ownership. A per-ticket mutex design would be another legitimate comparison.

Both branches use atomic storage in this shared harness. Atomics inside the mutex are not needed merely to protect that ticket's ownership when every access follows the same lock protocol. The shared representation makes the comparison convenient but contributes overhead of its own.

Other limits are visible in the program:

  • Every successful sale updates the shared sold counter.
  • Adjacent owners and adjacent per-worker statistics can share cache lines.
  • Random selection wastes more attempts near sellout because most tickets are already occupied.
  • The tests run sequentially; scheduling, cache state, and machine conditions affect timings.
  • Relaxed ordering is sufficient here for numeric ownership and counting; it is not a publication mechanism for unrelated ticket payload fields.

Do not infer that CAS is always faster. In the new validation run, each mode sold 100,000 tickets; the optimistic mode recorded six lost races. That is one observation, not a prediction for another machine or thread count.

Try: vary the thread count, then explain changes in attempts, occupied selections, races, and elapsed time separately. A high retry count is not by itself evidence of invalid results.

Four numbers, four different stories

A run might report a million attempts, almost a million occupied selections, 100,000 wins, and only a handful of lost races. That is not contradictory. An occupied selection notices a ticket already owned when it first checks. A lost race first observes a free ticket, then loses the conditional claim before its strong CAS commits. Most failed searches near sellout can belong to the first category without representing close simultaneous claims.

A mutex version has no comparable failed commit inside the protected check-and-set, but it can spend time waiting to reach that check. Reporting zero lost races does not mean it paid zero coordination cost. Conversely, a larger number of attempts does not mean it made more useful progress.

Why random searching gets expensive near the end

As a reasoning model, assume each selection independently chooses uniformly among N ticket IDs. After k tickets are taken, the chance that the next selection finds a free ticket is (N-k)/N. The expected number of selections until the next success is therefore N/(N-k) in that model.

The early successes are easy. The last free ticket has probability 1/N on each selection, so finding it takes N selections on average under those assumptions. Summing the stages gives N * (1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/N) expected selections to collect all tickets. This is an explanation of the search cost, not a performance prediction for our deterministic PRNG and real thread interleaving.

It tells us what to separate experimentally. Millions of occupied selections can arise from the workload itself, not just from the synchronization primitive. Replacing random search with a work-allocation strategy would change the experiment as well as its runtime.

The benchmark compares coordination granularity too

The global mutex serializes A claiming ticket 23 with B claiming ticket 81, even though the IDs are independent. Per-ticket CAS does not impose that same global exclusion. A per-ticket mutex would be another design worth comparing before concluding the entire difference is “mutex versus atomic.”

Both paths still update the shared sold count. Both use per-worker statistics whose adjacent storage can false-share. Both random streams and the loop's termination check contribute work. These are not defects in a small learning benchmark, but they limit the conclusion it can support.

Reader, condensed: Aren't both branches already using atomics? Does that make the comparison misleading?

Explanation: It makes the implementation convenient and the claim narrower. The important distinction is where the logical check-and-claim is coordinated. Some atomic accesses in the locked branch are redundant for its own exclusion protocol. A carefully controlled benchmark should state these choices rather than advertise one universal winner.

15. Correct executions and state machines

The learner's turning point was recognizing that correctness applies to every allowed execution, not merely the output observed in a few runs.

For one ticket, a valid transition is:

FREE → owned by A

An attempt by B after that transition must fail or follow an explicitly defined later ownership transition. It must not silently create a second owner. The state machine and ownership model define what synchronization has to preserve.

A mutex and CAS can implement the same abstract transition with different execution strategies. One controls entry to the decision. The other makes the commit conditional on the observed state still being current.

This also explains why a “whole database in memory” does not make either strategy universally preferable. Short private work, expensive external operations, read/write mix, conflict frequency, and retry cost all matter. A CDN label by itself is not enough to select a synchronization policy.

For an optimistic path, repeated computation must be safe to discard. Sending an email, charging a card, or writing to another independent system before validation is not undone merely because a later CAS fails. Such side effects need their own commitment and duplication rules.

A reusable reasoning checklist:

  1. What state does the decision depend on?
  2. Who may change that state?
  3. What transition is allowed?
  4. Where does the transition become accepted?
  5. What invalidates an observation made earlier?
  6. What happens after waiting, failed validation, or participant failure?

The next lab expands the relevant state from one ownership value to a relationship between multiple fields.

A valid execution is more than a plausible final value

Consider two workers that both report owning ticket 23 while the final owner array contains only worker B. The final array looks like a valid single-owner state. But A has already been told its claim succeeded. If success authorizes something outside that array, overwriting the owner does not retract A's permission. The accepted history, not just the final representation, matters.

That is why we identify a linearization point in these small examples: a point at which the abstract operation can be regarded as taking effect. For the CAS seat claim it is the successful conditional update. For the mutex-protected claim, we can choose the state change inside the protected operation because cooperating observers cannot interleave an incompatible operation there. The implementation may span many instructions while the abstract operation has one effect in the relevant history.

This is not a claim that every program using a mutex is automatically linearizable. Its operation boundary, returns, and observer protocol still need to fit the abstract behavior. An early return announcing success before the guarded change would require a different argument.

Work that can be thrown away, and work that cannot

Optimistic computation is easiest when it prepares a private result that can be discarded. Calculate a candidate configuration, validate the base version, then publish. If validation fails, the private result can be reconsidered.

An irreversible external effect is different. Suppose a worker charges a customer before its ticket CAS. If the CAS fails, discarding a local variable does not undo the charge. “Retry the transaction” may charge again. The system needs a design connecting the external effect to commitment, including whatever idempotency or deduplication its actual contract requires.

The course did not implement a general external transaction system, so we should not imply that a short CAS loop solves one. It supplied the reasoning habit: distinguish private speculative work, accepted local state, and effects outside the mechanism's control.

An ownership model can remove a local race

The later networking exercise will have many connections in progress but only one thread processing their events. That can give one owner of local node state. Multiple socket operations exist concurrently in time, yet local handlers do not execute simultaneously. Some mutex problems disappear by architecture rather than by adding a new lock.

The machines still run independently, however. A single-threaded node can receive stale leadership information and make a locally coherent but globally unsafe decision. Eliminating local data races does not establish a single authoritative history across those machines.

The progression is therefore not “keep adding synchronization until nothing can overlap.” It is to choose a state machine, identify its owners and acceptance rules, then use the mechanism appropriate to that scope.

16. Lab: consistent snapshots of multiple fields

Start with checking 500 and savings 500. Transfer 100 from checking to savings. Complete states must satisfy:

checking + savings == 1000
State Checking Savings Total
Before transfer 500 500 1000
After first field update 400 500 900
Complete transfer 400 600 1000

The intermediate 900 really can exist in memory during this implementation; it is not a completed transfer state. A reader can also combine values from different moments, such as old checking and new savings, and obtain 1100. Individual atomic accesses do not make the pair one snapshot.

Validate the version before accepting the snapshot

Use an even version for a stable state and an odd version while a writer updates it. Writers serialize with a mutex. Readers accept their collected fields only when the starting version is even and equals the ending version.

Edited source program — snapshot.c: nanosleep replaces usleep for POSIX feature-macro compatibility; a straightforward retry loop replaces the source's do/while; the retry counter is widened.

#define _POSIX_C_SOURCE 200809L
#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdatomic.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <time.h>

atomic_int checking = 500, savings = 500;
atomic_uint version = 0;
pthread_mutex_t writer_lock = PTHREAD_MUTEX_INITIALIZER;

void delay_ms(long ms) {
    struct timespec t = { .tv_sec = ms / 1000, .tv_nsec = (ms % 1000) * 1000000L };
    nanosleep(&t, NULL);
}

void *writer(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    pthread_mutex_lock(&writer_lock);
    atomic_fetch_add(&version, 1); // Odd: update in progress
    atomic_store(&checking, 400);
    delay_ms(100);
    atomic_store(&savings, 600);
    atomic_fetch_add(&version, 1); // Even: complete version
    pthread_mutex_unlock(&writer_lock);
    return NULL;
}

void *reader(void *arg) {
    (void)arg;
    unsigned long long retries = 0;
    for (;;) {
        unsigned before = atomic_load(&version);
        if (before & 1u) {
            retries++;
            continue;
        }
        int c = atomic_load(&checking);
        int s = atomic_load(&savings);
        unsigned after = atomic_load(&version);
        if (before != after) {
            retries++;
            continue;
        }
        printf("checking=%d savings=%d total=%d version=%u retries=%llu\n", c, s, c + s, after, retries);
        return NULL;
    }
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t w, r;
    pthread_create(&w, NULL, writer, NULL);
    delay_ms(20); // Encourages overlap; does not guarantee scheduling
    pthread_create(&r, NULL, reader, NULL);
    pthread_join(w, NULL);
    pthread_join(r, NULL);
    pthread_mutex_destroy(&writer_lock);
    return 0;
}

All shared fields in this listing are atomic, and their operations use the default sequentially consistent ordering. That is deliberately a simpler model than an optimized production sequence-lock implementation.

Do not replace the fields with ordinary unsynchronized C integers and argue that a later version mismatch makes the race harmless. Rejecting an observation afterward does not undo undefined behavior from a C data race. The earlier conceptual version-counter sketch is a reasoning aid, not permission for non-atomic concurrent payload access.

What is established, and under which assumptions?

A reader may accept the complete old state or the complete new state, depending on scheduling. The delay encourages overlap; it is not a scheduling guarantee. A new run accepted (400, 600) at version 2 and retried while the writer was active.

The argument relies on cooperating writers, serialization of writers, correct version updates, and no version wraparound between the reader's version checks. The one-update demonstration avoids wraparound. A general reusable implementation would need a bound or other treatment for it.

Readers can starve under sustained writing. A writer that stalls while the version is odd can prevent their progress. This example demonstrates snapshot validation rather than a general progress guarantee.

The new idea: correctness can come from rejecting an inconsistent observation before using it. Preventing every intermediate observation is not always necessary.

Try: walk through a reader that starts before the writer, reads one old field and one new field, then compares its two versions. Identify why it must reject that attempted snapshot.

Prove the snapshot under the actual operations used

Use the sequentially consistent ordering of this teaching program to reason about one total order of its atomic operations. A serialized writer makes the version odd before changing fields and even after finishing. A reader accepts only an even starting version that equals its ending version.

If a writer's update falls between the two version checks, the ending version must differ or indicate an update in progress, assuming it cannot wrap around and return to the starting value during that interval. If the writer finishes before the reader's first check, the reader can take the completed new fields. If it begins after the second check, the local copies already belong to the completed earlier version. The reader uses those local copies rather than rereading shared fields after validation.

The writer mutex is important even though readers do not take it. With two uncoordinated writers, one writer could increment an odd version to even while the other is still modifying data. “Even means stable” would then be false. The marker's meaning is a protocol invariant that writer serialization helps maintain.

Two distinct rejected observations

First, the reader may see an odd starting version. It need not read the payload at all; it retries because the protocol announces an active update. Second, it may begin at an even version, collect fields, and later discover a changed version. It rejects that attempted snapshot, including any mixed values it temporarily held.

The second case is the conceptual advance. The reader need not prevent the writer from running while it collects candidate values. It must refuse to use those values as a valid snapshot when the evidence fails.

That is safe in this listing because every payload access is itself atomic. A reader cannot perform undefined ordinary C races and retroactively legalize them by rejecting the result. Validation protects the higher-level meaning; it is not a time machine that repairs invalid language operations.

Read the retry loop's control flow carefully

The uploaded atomic_state_race.c uses continue inside a do/while. In C, that jumps to the loop condition, not directly to the top of the body. Its condition begins with (v1 & 1) || ..., so short-circuit evaluation avoids using v2 on the odd-version path before v2 has been assigned. That subtle fact matters when reviewing the original.

The teaching version uses for (;;) with explicit acceptance and retry paths, making that reasoning easier. It also uses nanosleep rather than relying on visibility of the older usleep declaration under a strict feature-macro setting, and widens a counter that can grow rapidly in a busy retry loop. These edits are documented; they are not a claim that the source's specific continue path necessarily reads an uninitialized v2.

Check: after a reader has copied a valid old version, does a writer starting immediately afterward invalidate those private copies? No. The claim is that they form a consistent version, not that they remain the most recent values forever.

17. When the validation protocol cannot be trusted

The version counter works because every writer follows its rules. A writer that changes a balance without changing the version can fool readers that trust the counter. This is a change in assumptions, not a failure of the atomic load instruction.

A Byzantine participant can behave arbitrarily: lie, withhold a response, or give contradictory answers to different parties. Malicious intent is one possible cause; arbitrary malfunction is another.

The first replica exercise made three replicas return (400, 600) and a faulty replica return (300, 700). Both reports preserve the total of 1000, but only the former executes the requested transfer of 100.

The coordinator chose the earliest recorded response. That rule can accept the wrong transition even though all thread accesses are synchronized.

Source program — first-reply.c:

#define _POSIX_C_SOURCE 200809L

#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <time.h>

#define REPLICAS 4

struct Account {
    int checking;
    int savings;
};

struct Reply {
    int replica_id;
    struct Account state;
    struct timespec arrived;
};

struct Reply replies[REPLICAS];

void delay_ms(long ms) {
    struct timespec t = { .tv_sec = ms / 1000, .tv_nsec = (ms % 1000) * 1000000L };
    nanosleep(&t, NULL);
}

void *replica(void *arg) {
    int id = *(int *)arg;
    struct Account local = {500, 500};

    if (id == 3) {
        // Byzantine replica: deliberately reports a false transition
        local.checking = 300;
        local.savings = 700;
        delay_ms(1);
    } else {
        // Honest replicas execute the requested $100 transfer
        local.checking -= 100;
        local.savings += 100;
        delay_ms(50 + id * 10);
    }

    replies[id].replica_id = id;
    replies[id].state = local;
    clock_gettime(CLOCK_MONOTONIC, &replies[id].arrived);

    return NULL;
}

int arrived_before(struct timespec a, struct timespec b) {
    if (a.tv_sec != b.tv_sec) return a.tv_sec < b.tv_sec;
    return a.tv_nsec < b.tv_nsec;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t threads[REPLICAS];
    int ids[REPLICAS];

    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++) {
        ids[i] = i;
        pthread_create(&threads[i], NULL, replica, &ids[i]);
    }

    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++)
        pthread_join(threads[i], NULL);

    // FLAW: trust whichever replica responded first
    int winner = 0;

    for (int i = 1; i < REPLICAS; i++) {
        if (arrived_before(replies[i].arrived, replies[winner].arrived))
            winner = i;
    }

    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++) {
        printf("Replica %d: checking=%d savings=%d total=%d\n",
            i, replies[i].state.checking, replies[i].state.savings,
            replies[i].state.checking + replies[i].state.savings);
    }

    struct Account accepted = replies[winner].state;

    printf("\nAccepted replica: %d\n", winner);
    printf("Final checking:  %d\n", accepted.checking);
    printf("Final savings:   %d\n", accepted.savings);

    return 0;
}

Each worker writes a separate reply element. The coordinator joins all workers before reading their replies. Adding a mutex to these accesses does not repair an acceptance rule that trusts the wrong evidence.

The source's objection was productive: this exercise mostly demonstrated bad result selection, rather than a new shared-state concurrency problem. Comparing compatible replies or calculating the expected transition locally would address that demonstration. It did not yet expose competing decisions.

The redesigned exercise therefore introduces two individually valid proposals for one log position. This brings local synchronization back into the problem while giving replica agreement a separate responsibility.

Takeaway: internal consistency, correct execution of a requested operation, and agreement between participants are distinct properties.

A total of 1000 does not identify the requested operation

The first replica example preserves an accounting invariant even in its faulty response. Both 400/600 and 300/700 total 1000. Only one is the result of moving exactly 100 from an initial 500/500 state. An invariant that rules out some bad results need not characterize every valid transition completely.

This is a useful discipline in designing checks. A checksum, a conserved quantity, a type check, or agreement between replicas can establish one property without establishing every property the application cares about. Ask what alternative histories still satisfy the check.

In this toy example, a coordinator that knows the starting balances and the requested transfer could calculate the expected answer itself. That is why the learner objected that merely comparing predetermined reports was not yet the intended concurrency problem. The redesign introduced conflicting proposals so the votes had something genuinely contested to decide.

Trust cannot be repaired by scheduling alone

The version counter assumes writers announce changes correctly. A faulty writer that changes the data without changing the version can defeat that evidence. Adding more atomic operations to the reader does not force the writer to obey the contract.

The Byzantine model permits arbitrary behavior by the faulty participant. Within the simulator, however, that participant is represented by a deliberately special-cased function in one address space. We are assuming the rest of the process's memory and code are not arbitrarily overwritten by that function. The exercise models one aspect of Byzantine messages; it does not isolate a malicious C thread that has access to all process memory.

This boundary deserves to be explicit because otherwise “we tolerate a liar” could sound like a memory-security claim the simulator does not supply. Independent processes or machines, stable identities, and appropriate message validation introduce additional boundaries in a real deployment.

Agreement is also not a truth oracle

If three replicas correctly execute an agreed deterministic transition on valid input, their compatible reports can be useful evidence under a fault model. If all three share an application bug or are fed a false real-world input, agreement alone does not prove the outside-world fact was true.

The course will use quorum intersection to establish the impossibility of two conflicting accepted decisions under specified rules. That is a precise agreement property. It should not be inflated into “the majority is always right” or “authentication proves that a signed claim is truthful.” A signature can identify an endorser while that endorser deliberately lies.

18. Lab: conflicting proposals and accidental double voting

One account starts with 500. Proposal A requests a payment of 300; B requests 400. Either can be accepted from the initial balance, but both cannot be honored together. Neither proposal is inherently “the bad one.” They conflict for the same decision slot.

There are four replicas. R0–R2 are intended to vote once for this position. R3 is faulty and approves both. A proposal is treated as committed after three distinct replica approvals.

Intentionally flawed source program — bft_flawed.c:

#define _POSIX_C_SOURCE 200809L

#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdatomic.h>
#include <stdio.h>

#define REPLICAS 4
#define BYZANTINE 3
#define QUORUM 3
#define UNVOTED -1

struct Replica {
    atomic_int voted_for;
    pthread_barrier_t barrier;
};

struct Proposal {
    int id;
    int amount;
    int approvals;
    int votes[REPLICAS];
};

struct Replica replicas[REPLICAS];

int request_vote(int replica_id, int proposal) {
    if (replica_id == BYZANTINE)
        return 1; // Faulty replica approves both proposals

    struct Replica *r = &replicas[replica_id];

    int observed = atomic_load(&r->voted_for);

    // Force both proposers to reach this point
    pthread_barrier_wait(&r->barrier);

    if (observed == UNVOTED) {
        atomic_store(&r->voted_for, proposal);
        return 1;
    }

    return observed == proposal;
}

void *propose(void *arg) {
    struct Proposal *p = arg;

    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++) {
        p->votes[i] = request_vote(i, p->id);

        if (p->votes[i])
            p->approvals++;
    }

    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    pthread_t threads[2];

    struct Proposal a = { .id = 0, .amount = 300 };
    struct Proposal b = { .id = 1, .amount = 400 };

    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++) {
        atomic_init(&replicas[i].voted_for, UNVOTED);

        if (i != BYZANTINE)
            pthread_barrier_init(&replicas[i].barrier, NULL, 2);
    }

    pthread_create(&threads[0], NULL, propose, &a);
    pthread_create(&threads[1], NULL, propose, &b);

    pthread_join(threads[0], NULL);
    pthread_join(threads[1], NULL);

    printf("\n--- REPLICA VOTES ---\n");

    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++) {
        printf("R%d: A=%s B=%s\n", i,
            a.votes[i] ? "YES" : "NO",
            b.votes[i] ? "YES" : "NO");
    }

    int commit_a = a.approvals >= QUORUM;
    int commit_b = b.approvals >= QUORUM;

    printf("\n--- CONSENSUS RESULTS ---\n");
    printf("A: %d votes -> %s\n", a.approvals, commit_a ? "COMMIT" : "REJECT");
    printf("B: %d votes -> %s\n", b.approvals, commit_b ? "COMMIT" : "REJECT");

    if (commit_a && commit_b) {
        printf("\nSAFETY FAILURE: conflicting transactions finalized!\n");
        printf("Balance if both honored: $%d\n", 500 - a.amount - b.amount);
    }

    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++) {
        if (i != BYZANTINE)
            pthread_barrier_destroy(&replicas[i].barrier);
    }

    return 0;
}

The atomic object does not make the vote operation atomic

At each honest replica, both proposer threads load UNVOTED. The barrier forces them both to reach that point before either continues. Each then records its proposal and returns approval.

Step Proposer A Proposer B
Observe Reads UNVOTED Reads UNVOTED
Rendezvous Waits at replica barrier Waits at replica barrier
Decide Stores A, returns YES Stores B, returns YES

Only one value remains in voted_for, but both callers have received a YES. The final stored value does not erase an approval already returned.

The new validation run reproduced four approvals for A and four for B. The program printed its safety failure and a hypothetical downstream balance of -200. It models vote decisions; it does not actually perform bank payments.

The requirement is one honest replica must not approve conflicting proposals for the same slot. Synchronization has to protect that entire decision, not just each load or store individually.

Returning YES creates information outside voted_for

The flawed implementation is especially instructive because inspecting the last stored vote can conceal the failure. A and B both observe UNVOTED, then each stores its ID and returns YES. The object eventually contains one ID, but there are now two positive responses in the proposal records.

The property we need is about the accepted history: one honest replica may not authorize both conflicting proposals for this slot. It is not merely “the integer stores one integer at a time.” Atomic storage gives the latter trivial property. The check-and-record operation must give the former.

The replica barrier deliberately makes this difference visible. Both handlers must finish the atomic load before either can pass the barrier. That guarantees the shared premise in the flawed first round without introducing a C data race on the atomic vote. It is a controlled algorithmic failure.

A quorum needs distinct participants

Suppose a proposer retries the same request three times and receives YES from R0 each time. Those are not three independent replica approvals. A certificate or quorum tally must account for identities and the relevant instance/slot, round, and proposal. Repeated transport messages do not create new voters.

Our array representation makes that easy to see: each replica has one slot in a proposal's votes array, and approvals are collected from distinct indices. Networking will make this bookkeeping more explicit because duplicate responses and retries can occur.

Why two quorums overlap in an honest replica

Each quorum contains three of the four replicas. Even if the first contains R0/R1/R3 and the second R1/R2/R3, they overlap at R1 and R3. With at most one arbitrary faulty participant, at least one of those two is honest.

If the honest member refuses conflicting approvals under the protocol's rules, the two conflicting quorums cannot both be valid. Notice how the mathematics depends on a local behavior rule. If a bug lets honest R1 approve both, counting to three does not rescue the design. This is exactly what the flawed program demonstrates.

Nor is the number three a universal rule for every distributed experiment. Three-of-four here is the one-fault Byzantine-inspired setting. Later we use two-of-three as an election majority in a crash-fault learning model. The numbers come from different assumptions and should not be interchanged because both examples use the word “quorum.”

Exercise: if one faulty replica signs both A and B, does it alone create two three-vote certificates? No. It still needs two additional distinct endorsements for each. The intersection argument explains why the honest rules matter.

19. Repairing the right critical section

The source explored three useful stages before reaching the correct repair.

Stage A: a global mutex is a valid repair

The learner removed the forcing barrier and put a common mutex around each call to request_vote. This serializes the entire check-and-record operation, so it prevents accidental honest double voting in this simulation.

Its cost is scope: requests to R0 and R2 wait on the same lock even though they concern independent replica state. This is the ticket-table comparison appearing again.

Stage B: a per-replica lock with an early unlock is still broken

Moving to one mutex per replica permits independent replicas to work in parallel. But this attempted placement leaves the decision outside the lock:

pthread_mutex_lock(&r->lock);
int observed = atomic_load(&r->voted_for);
pthread_mutex_unlock(&r->lock);

if (observed == UNVOTED) {
    atomic_store(&r->voted_for, proposal);
    return 1;
}

A can read UNVOTED and unlock. B can then read UNVOTED and unlock. Both can subsequently approve. A correct-looking run merely missed that schedule.

Stage C: extend the lock, then release it on every path

The repaired critical section covers observation, decision, and state change. It computes the response, unlocks, then returns. Moving the unlock below an early return would instead leave the mutex locked forever.

The other deadlock encountered in this progression was the two-party barrier inside an exclusive lock. The owner waits at the barrier for a participant that cannot enter because the owner holds the mutex.

Also preserve the API correction:

pthread_mutex_init(&replicas[i].lock, NULL);

The second argument is an attributes pointer or NULL. PTHREAD_PROCESS_PRIVATE is an attribute value, not that pointer.

Complete consolidated repair

This teaching version combines the already-discussed faulty behaviors into three modes. It removes unused barriers, uses ordinary int replica state under the mutex, and destroys the mutexes after both threads finish. Each proposal structure is owned by its proposer thread; main reads it after join.

It does not add recovery rounds or solve the next split-vote challenge.

#include <stdio.h>
#include <string.h>

#define BYZANTINE 3
#define QUORUM 3
#define UNVOTED -1

enum { DOUBLE_VOTE, WITHHOLD, OPPOSITE };
int fault_mode = DOUBLE_VOTE;

struct Replica {
    int voted_for;
    pthread_mutex_t lock;
};

struct Proposal {
    int id;
    int amount;
    int approvals;
    int votes[REPLICAS];
};

struct Replica replicas[REPLICAS];

int request_vote(int replica_id, int proposal) {
    if (replica_id == BYZANTINE)
        return fault_mode == DOUBLE_VOTE;
    struct Replica *r = &replicas[replica_id];
    pthread_mutex_lock(&r->lock);
    int observed = r->voted_for;
    int approved = (observed == UNVOTED || observed == proposal);
    if (observed == UNVOTED)
        r->voted_for = proposal;
    pthread_mutex_unlock(&r->lock);
    return approved;

    struct Proposal *p = arg;
    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++) {

            p->votes[i] = !p->votes[i - 1];
        else
            p->votes[i] = request_vote(i, p->id);
        p->approvals += p->votes[i];
    }
    return NULL;
}

    printf("A: %d votes -> %s\n", a.approvals, commit_a ? "COMMIT" : "REJECT");
    printf("B: %d votes -> %s\n", b.approvals, commit_b ? "COMMIT" : "REJECT");
    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++)
        pthread_mutex_destroy(&replicas[i].lock);
    return commit_a && commit_b;
}

Using an ordinary int is safe here because all concurrent honest-replica accesses use that replica's mutex. Thread creation publishes the initial state before the workers run; joining them precedes cleanup.

Why three approvals protect this one decision slot

Two sets of three distinct replicas chosen from four overlap in at least:

3 + 3 - 4 = 2 replicas

At most one is Byzantine, so at least one overlapping member must be honest. If both conflicting proposals committed, that honest member would have had to approve both. The repaired local rule prevents that.

This is a safety argument under the toy model's assumptions: distinct stable replica identities, no forged votes, at most one faulty participant, and honest votes remembered for this slot. Repeated tests complement that argument; they do not replace it.

In a real distributed deployment, local array indices and function returns would not supply authentication, independent failure isolation, persistent votes after crashes, or safe protocol changes between rounds. The simulator is BFT-inspired; it is not a complete BFT protocol.

The global mutex solution was legitimate

The learner initially described the outer mutex solution as perhaps “not allowed.” But the relevant question is what it establishes. With the forcing barrier removed and both proposer threads using the same mutex around the whole request, the honest replica's decision cannot be interrupted in the forbidden check/store interval. That is a valid local repair in this model.

Moving the lock into each replica improves the scope of exclusion. It lets an A→R0 request overlap a B→R2 request while still serializing two requests for R0. The refinement preserves the proof for conflicting accesses and reduces coordination between unrelated state.

This is the same architectural lesson as the ticket table. “One global lock works” and “one global lock unnecessarily serializes independent operations” can both be true. Correctness is not invalidated merely because a more scalable design exists.

Two repairs that go wrong in different ways

The early-unlock attempt has a safety problem. It leaves the conditional state change outside protection. A thread can hold a locally cached UNVOTED observation after another thread is allowed to make the same observation. Both later approve.

The early-return attempt has a progress problem. Extending the critical section while leaving return 1 before the unlock can make the owner leave without releasing the mutex. The other thread then waits indefinitely. These are not the same defect and should not receive the same explanation.

One way to make the repaired function easier to audit is to have one exit point after the unlock. Compute approved, optionally change the vote, unlock, and return that computed result. This makes every branch's ownership lifetime visible in the function's compact shape.

Protect the predicate's scope, not the print statement

A printf placed under the lock can produce reassuringly ordered logs while the actual decision remains outside it. Conversely, moving or removing the print may expose a bad schedule that was already allowed. Logging is evidence about events reached by that execution, not the ownership rule itself.

The uploaded bft_fixed.c includes the now-correct full decision scope, but still carries unused barriers and the PTHREAD_PROCESS_PRIVATE argument at initialization. On implementations where that macro is zero, the latter may happen to behave like a null pointer constant. It is still the wrong API expression to teach; use NULL for default mutex attributes. The intent and portability of the call should not depend on an incidental macro value.

Review exercise: draw a line at every return in a lock-owning function. For each, ask whether this path still owns the lock. Then inspect every piece of state used in the decision and ask whether it can change after its protection ends. These two passes find different classes of defect.

20. Safety without progress

A system can avoid contradictory commits while being unable to commit anything.

Property Question Current toy protocol
Safety Can both conflicting proposals commit? Prevented under the stated one-slot assumptions
Liveness Will an eligible proposal eventually commit? Not guaranteed

If R3 withholds its vote and the honest replicas split A/B/A, A has two approvals and B has one. Repeating the same requests does not help because the honest replicas remember their votes.

Withholding alone does not force a stall. If all three honest replicas support A, A still reaches the three-vote threshold without R3.

The learner's opposite-vote experiment

The latest code makes R3 return the opposite of R2 for each proposal:

p->votes[i] = !p->votes[i - 1];

For each proposal, R2 and R3 contribute exactly one approval between them. The remaining distinction is what happened at R0 and R1.

Replica A in a stalled example B in that example
R0 YES NO
R1 NO YES
R2 NO YES
R3, opposite R2 YES NO
Total 2 2

If R0 and R1 instead support the same proposal, that proposal gets their two approvals plus the one supplied by R2/R3: three approvals. Thus the opposite-vote rule permits a 2–2 split; it does not guarantee either a stall or a winner for every schedule.

Both proposer loops visit replicas in the same order. In ordinary runs, one thread may finish several requests before the other advances. Per-replica locking permits different winners at different replicas; it does not force that interleaving to occur in the runs observed.

Why retrying or resetting is not a recovery protocol

Retrying unchanged requests obtains unchanged remembered votes. Resetting everyone to UNVOTED creates a different risk: an earlier vote may already have contributed to a valid decision, while another participant or delayed message still carries evidence of it.

Safe progress across attempts requires rules for retaining commitments and interpreting old evidence. Rounds/views, leaders, and quorum certificates were introduced as future concepts. Their implementation is beyond the completed progression in this source snapshot.

Halting can preserve safety vacuously

A system that approves nothing cannot approve two conflicting decisions. That is why the word “safe” alone is not enough to describe a useful service. We also need a progress claim with assumptions about available participants, message delivery, and scheduling.

The first attempted guaranteed stall rejected every odd-numbered replica and skipped the faulty one. That left only two participants able to approve, while the threshold was three. The outcome was explained by arithmetic, not by a subtle collective split among three functioning honest replicas. The experiment changed the population of participants on which the intended fault assumption relied.

The target experiment is more informative: all three honest replicas follow their vote-once rule, but their valid decisions divide between A and B. The faulty fourth withholds approval. A gets two and B one, or the reverse. The local proof works, yet the protocol has no rule ensuring a common winner.

Opposing one replica is not the same as opposing the whole quorum

The opposite-to-R2 behavior contributes one YES between R2 and R3 for each proposal. Whether a proposal reaches three then depends on the other two honest replicas. If R0 and R1 agree, their two approvals plus that one make three. If they disagree, each proposal gets one from R0/R1 and one from R2/R3, making a two–two split.

The learner's initial runs often showed one proposer sweeping the honest replicas. That was a permitted schedule, not a rule imposed by the per-replica mutexes. Making different outcomes reliable requires controlling the schedule at appropriate boundaries, not concluding the unobserved outcome is impossible.

Progress failure needs a stated time model

The one-shot toy permanently records its votes. An ordinary retry of the same requests has no reason to change them, so a failed round can remain stalled forever. More generally, a slow response is not automatically a failed participant. A timeout says the observer has not received the event within a chosen interval; it is not direct evidence of what every other participant did.

The next chapters make both points concrete: first by constructing a split without undefined behavior, then by distinguishing another local attempt from recovery when different observers have incomplete information. The old outline stopped before those experiments. The expanded source now gives us the next part of the course.

21. A reliable split without undefined behavior

The next task was to produce a split while keeping all three honest replicas correct. Random sleeps made it more likely but rarely made it dependable. A useful test should arrange the relevant interleaving without introducing an unrelated C data race.

One attempted scheduling mechanism used a global ready_to_proceed flag and a condition variable while waiting under different per-replica mutexes. It exposed the desired voting pattern, but did not give that pattern a reliable synchronization explanation. The flag's accesses did not all use the same protection. Concurrent waits on one condition variable also cannot simply use unrelated associated mutexes.

The distinction is important: intentionally making request order nondeterministic is legitimate. Intentionally allowing undefined behavior makes it difficult to know which phenomenon the experiment measures.

Synchronize stages, not the contested decision

Use different request orders for the two proposers, with one shared stage barrier after each request:

Stage A requests B requests What the gate establishes
1 R0 R1 Both initial claims finish before stage 2
2 R1 R0 Both encounter the other's recorded first claim
3 R2 R2 The remaining honest vote is a real concurrent contest

The stage-one requests target different honest replicas, so both can succeed. The barrier then prevents either proposer from running ahead to collect the other's initial replica before that first claim exists. R2 remains contested; either proposer can win it. With R3 withholding approval, the result is two votes versus one, never the required three.

Additional controlled teaching experiment — split_vote.c: this is an editorial consolidation of the scheduling idea developed in the thread. It is not presented as a byte-for-byte copy of the learner's final source.

#define _POSIX_C_SOURCE 200809L
#include <assert.h>
#include <pthread.h>
#include <stdio.h>

#define REPLICAS 4
#define QUORUM 3
#define UNVOTED -1

struct Replica {
    int voted_for;
    pthread_mutex_t lock;
} replicas[REPLICAS];

struct Proposal {
    int id;
    int votes[REPLICAS];
    int approvals;
};

pthread_barrier_t step_gate;

int request_vote(int replica, int proposal) {
    if (replica == 3) return 0; // One faulty participant withholds its vote
    struct Replica *r = &replicas[replica];
    pthread_mutex_lock(&r->lock);
    int approved = (r->voted_for == UNVOTED || r->voted_for == proposal);
    if (r->voted_for == UNVOTED) r->voted_for = proposal;
    pthread_mutex_unlock(&r->lock);
    return approved;
}

void *propose(void *arg) {
    struct Proposal *p = arg;
    const int orders[2][3] = {{0, 1, 2}, {1, 0, 2}};
    for (int step = 0; step < 3; step++) {
        int replica = orders[p->id][step];
        p->votes[replica] = request_vote(replica, p->id);
        p->approvals += p->votes[replica];
        pthread_barrier_wait(&step_gate); // Same stage gate; no replica lock held
    }
    return NULL;
}

int main(void) {
    struct Proposal a = {.id = 0}, b = {.id = 1};
    pthread_t ta, tb;
    pthread_barrier_init(&step_gate, NULL, 2);
    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++) {
        replicas[i].voted_for = UNVOTED;
        pthread_mutex_init(&replicas[i].lock, NULL);
    }
    pthread_create(&ta, NULL, propose, &a);
    pthread_create(&tb, NULL, propose, &b);
    pthread_join(ta, NULL);
    pthread_join(tb, NULL);
    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++)
        printf("R%d: A=%d B=%d\n", i, a.votes[i], b.votes[i]);
    printf("A=%d B=%d quorum=%d\n", a.approvals, b.approvals, QUORUM);
    assert(a.approvals < QUORUM && b.approvals < QUORUM);
    assert(a.approvals + b.approvals == 3);
    assert(a.votes[0] == 1 && b.votes[1] == 1);
    for (int i = 0; i < 3; i++) assert(a.votes[i] + b.votes[i] == 1);
    for (int i = 0; i < REPLICAS; i++) pthread_mutex_destroy(&replicas[i].lock);
    pthread_barrier_destroy(&step_gate);
    return 0;
}

Why one stage barrier matters

Do not replace the shared gate with “whichever replica's barrier this worker just visited” after giving workers different orders. A could wait at R0's barrier while B waits at R1's. Each barrier expects two participants, and neither can reach the other's barrier. That would be a deadlock of the test harness rather than the intended lack of a commit quorum.

Similarly, do not hold a replica's exclusive mutex while waiting for the other proposer to enter that same replica. The first participant would be waiting for progress it has itself excluded.

The actual source also encountered if (n > i > 0). C parses this as (n > i) > 0; it is not mathematical chained-comparison notation. If a range is intended, write both comparisons with &&. A misleading condition can accidentally put a barrier on every iteration, changing the test's schedule.

A condition variable waits for a predicate

A conventional pattern looks like this:

pthread_mutex_lock(&gate_lock);
while (!may_proceed)
    pthread_cond_wait(&changed, &gate_lock);
/* The predicate is true while the required lock is held. */
pthread_mutex_unlock(&gate_lock);

The actor changing may_proceed participates in that same mutex protocol. The wait releases the mutex while blocked and reacquires it before returning. The loop matters because a wakeup does not by itself prove the predicate is true. A condition variable is a way to wait for protected state to change; it is not an extra Boolean-valued atomic instruction.

Check your understanding: could you get the same split by making two replicas permanently refuse? Yes, but it would establish a different result: there are fewer potential approvals than the threshold. The controlled experiment establishes failure of progress despite all three honest replicas making valid, usable decisions.

22. Another round is not a vote on who was right

After producing a failed round, the learner added retries and reset voting state. This introduced a surprisingly difficult language problem: what does it mean to make the nodes “agree” next time?

We need to separate three decisions:

  • Which proposal is valid for the application?
  • Which eligible proposal will occupy this particular decision slot?
  • Which participants, if any, have violated the protocol?

A disagreement between honest replicas does not answer the third question. R0 may receive A first while R1 receives B first. Both can follow the rules. Nor does a two–one distribution establish that the two-vote proposal is the inherently correct one. It is information about this attempt's ordering.

Work through the learner's two possible outcomes

Suppose a failed round has A/A/B among the honest replicas and an abstention from the faulty one. No proposal has three approvals. In a simplified, fully observed test with no earlier commitment or other protocol restriction, a later attempt could validly choose A or B.

Replica Failed round Later outcome 1 Later outcome 2
R0 A A B
R1 A A B
R2 B A B
R3 Abstains Abstains Abstains
Accepted result None A B

The aim is not to punish R2 for its earlier minority vote. The aim is to establish one safe decision. We might reduce contention by arranging one proposer for a later attempt, but the rule for choosing a safe proposal has to be defined when previous evidence can constrain the choice.

Why the reset experiments were still useful

The learner's first retry loop repeatedly observed identical votes because replica state had not been reinitialized in the relevant scope. Resetting only one variable does not necessarily reset proposal totals, vote arrays, or per-replica remembered decisions. The round's state needs a clear owner and initialization boundary.

After the workers finish and the centralized coordinator observes a failed attempt, it can reset the toy and run another attempt. Removing a forcing barrier in the later round lets one proposer get ahead; that can make a quorum common. It is an observation about a favorable schedule, not a proof that unlimited repetition guarantees progress.

Every worker also has to reach each required phase gate. If only the failed proposer enters a two-party barrier while the successful one exits, the barrier waits forever. Logging “waiting for barrier” can therefore describe a real control-flow mismatch rather than an atomic state that “needs more resetting.” The required participant set is part of the protocol.

The question that corrected the assignment

Reader, condensed: If three already agreed and the program knows that, why keep voting in another round?

Explanation: In this centralized simulation, there is no reason. Main joins the handlers, inspects the complete result, and can stop when it has the required quorum. The source eventually corrected the exercise: it had been asking for a danger that the current model did not actually expose.

Different program executions choosing different winners are not contradictory commits within one instance. Each invocation starts from its own initialized state. A/A/A in one run and B/B/B in a different run do not violate the one-decision property for either run.

This correction is part of the lesson, not incidental conversational noise. A more sophisticated mechanism must answer a problem present in the model. Before adding history-preservation machinery, introduce the incomplete knowledge that makes that history difficult to observe.

23. Quorum evidence and incomplete knowledge

Consider the same actual votes: R0, R1, and R2 endorsed A; R3 abstained. Now let two independent coordinators receive different subsets of the responses.

Evidence from round 0 Coordinator X Coordinator Y
R0's A approval Received Received
R1's A approval Received Received
R2's A approval Received Delayed or missing
What can be concluded locally? Three approvals satisfy this toy's commit rule Only two approvals have been observed

Y has not proved that no quorum exists. It lacks a piece of evidence that X already possesses. A timeout changes Y's knowledge about elapsed waiting, not the actual fact of whether R2 voted or X collected the vote.

This is the missing ingredient from the centralized join-and-count simulation. There, main can wait for all completed thread handlers and inspect their records. Here, no observer automatically receives a complete snapshot of another process's messages and decisions.

What could go wrong with a naive new round?

Suppose X has enough evidence to finalize A for slot 42, but becomes unavailable before Y learns that fact. Y initiates a new round. If the protocol simply erases every vote and permits R0/R1/R2 to approve B, Y can collect a new three-vote set for B in the same slot.

Each round separately obeyed “one vote per replica in that round.” Each quorum contained three distinct participants. The overlapping honest replicas changed their votes between rounds. Thus the one-round intersection argument alone did not establish cross-round safety.

The new requirement is a rule constraining what those honest participants may endorse after prior voting evidence. Production protocols use more structure, such as phases, retained locks, and information exchanged during changes of leadership. The exact safe rule depends on the protocol. Adding an integer named round does not supply it automatically.

History is a constraint, not a popularity ranking

The dialogue repeatedly returned to “subject to preserving earlier commitments.” The careful interpretation is:

Historical fact Implication for this same decision slot
A received more votes in an uncommitted attempt Does not alone make A the required winner
A has been finalized under the protocol A conflicting result cannot later be finalized
This node has not seen a finalization Does not prove no other node has sufficient evidence
Earlier protocol evidence imposes a safety restriction Follow the restriction even if no final decision is locally known

A real protocol may need to preserve evidence before finalization precisely because another participant could know more. “No commitment in my local records” is weaker than “a protocol proof establishes that this new proposal cannot conflict with anything already decided.”

Keep instance, round, and term distinct

A slot or instance identifies the decision being made. A round/view identifies an attempt or coordinator epoch within the protocol. A term in the later leadership exercise identifies a leadership epoch. Moving to a new attempt is not permission to contradict an earlier binding decision for the same slot. Nor is choosing a different value for a new independent slot necessarily a contradiction.

Reasoning exercise: X has A's certificate; Y has only two A votes. What must Y not infer from its timeout? That A is known uncommitted. What additional problem must recovery solve? Gathering and preserving sufficient evidence so that incomplete knowledge cannot authorize an incompatible decision.

The source developed this model conceptually, then moved to independent machines. It did not complete a Byzantine view-change protocol. That boundary remains explicit in this edition.

24. A supervisor chooses a leader; an old node does not learn it

The course then moved from shared globals to three Linux C nodes contacted by a Python supervisor on Windows. This was a useful architectural change: each node had its own memory and received only the messages actually delivered to it. A global mutex could no longer make their observations identical.

The supervisor monitored response times and chose a preferred node, with a rolling measurement window and a threshold intended to avoid excessive switching. This is a useful selection policy for a controlled experiment. Fastest recent response is not itself evidence of exclusive authority to make future system decisions.

The small command vocabulary separated observations from actions: ping the node, inspect its status, announce a term and leader, and send a job tagged with a term. Nodes checked jobs against their local remembered leadership.

The local rule can be correct and still be insufficient

The relevant acceptance condition resembled:

if (request_term == node->term && node->leader_id == node->id) {
    /* Accept local work. */
}

Imagine R2 learned that it led term 1. Later the supervisor replaces it with R1 for term 2, but the announcement to R2 is withheld.

Node Local term Local leader Response to a matching local-term job
R1 2 R1 Accepts
R2 1 R2 Also accepts

The source reported this stale-leader behavior on the learner's LAN after selective announcement loss and network delay. It is not a C memory race. Each node can evaluate its local predicate accurately while the two predicates express incompatible beliefs about current authority.

The reported job only changed local node state. Accepting two local jobs is not yet proof of two contradictory entries committed to a replicated log. It demonstrates the authority gap that a later commitment mechanism must prevent from authorizing conflicting effects.

Verify that the fault injector caused the intended fault

One experimental result was obscured by the supervisor's own announce function. A loop printed that an announcement was dropped, but a separate loop sent the announcement to all nodes anyway. The log described intended fault injection rather than what the network-send path actually did.

Combining the skip decision with the send loop fixed that discrepancy. This is the distributed version of the earlier logging lesson: a printed interpretation is not sufficient evidence that the intended event occurred. Check the path performing the action.

What latency and silence can tell us

A larger response time may reflect load, scheduling, buffering, or a network path. A missing response may reflect an unreachable node, a lost request, a lost reply, or an action that happened without its reply being observed. The observer cannot generally infer the remote operation's exact history from silence alone.

A timeout is still useful: it lets a system stop waiting indefinitely and attempt another course of action. The new course of action must be safe under the uncertainty that remains. “We stopped waiting for the old leader” and “the old leader is incapable of acting” are not equivalent statements.

Check: can another supervisor message force an isolated node to change its belief? Not while that message cannot reach it. A robust design must prevent unsafe accepted effects even when some beliefs remain stale.

25. Election authority and permission to commit work

The next proposed step removed the Windows machine's ability to unilaterally appoint leaders. It could still measure, suggest candidates, and inject failures; the Linux nodes would need to authorize leadership through peer votes.

For a three-node crash-fault learning model, a candidate could seek two votes, including its own. An honest node would cast at most one vote in a term. The local state might include current_term, voted_for, and leader_id. Those fields need transition rules, not merely declarations.

Why one vote per term is useful

Two groups of two drawn from three nodes overlap in at least one node. If all participants follow the rule, that overlapping node cannot authorize two candidates in the same term. This is an election-safety argument for that model. It assumes stable identities and that the remembered vote is not forgotten in a way the model allows candidates to exploit.

If crash recovery is included, persistence becomes part of the problem. A process that restarts and forgets its vote may vote again in the same term. An in-memory integer is enough only for a model whose failure assumptions exclude that history or otherwise handle it. The source had proposed the peer-election rules, not finished this recovery implementation.

Three nodes and a two-node majority here do not supply the earlier three-of-four Byzantine guarantee. The crash-fault experiment and the Byzantine-inspired simulator must keep their failure assumptions separate.

A newly elected leader need not instantly correct every node

R0 and R1 may communicate and authorize leadership in a newer term while R2 remains isolated with its old view. That temporary disagreement is not itself proof that the election mechanism failed. The central requirement is what work can be accepted as authoritative despite the stale view.

Question Mechanism whose contract must answer it
Who may claim leadership in this term? Election votes and term-transition rules
Can a stale leader still commit new work? Commitment acknowledgements, retained history, and resource-enforcement rules
Can a node act forever without renewal? Any chosen lease/expiry protocol and its timing assumptions
Can a resource reject a stale actor? A fencing rule enforced at that resource

These rows are design questions, not a completed implementation. A local comparison between a job's term and a node's own term cannot tell an isolated node that a higher term now exists elsewhere.

A lease can limit authority in time under its actual clock and timing assumptions. A fencing token can let a resource reject older authority after it has accepted a newer token. Neither concept works merely because the sender includes an integer: the actor authorizing the lease or enforcing the fence must follow the necessary rules. Quorum authorization of work likewise needs correct log/commit semantics, not just a count of arbitrary replies.

The next protocol exercise remains open

The learner chose to implement transport and event handling before peer voting. That is a sensible dependency: an election handler cannot be tested cleanly while the server drops commands before reading them.

For a future implementation, predict the behavior of two simultaneous candidates in the same term. Explain which local transition prevents double voting, how newer terms affect existing state, and what survives a restart. Then separately ask whether a previously authorized leader can still cause external work. Election safety alone is not the complete state-machine replication argument.

26. From an accepted socket to a complete command

The uploaded serving.c establishes a listening socket, accepts connections, and closes them. It contains no application-level reply protocol. A successful TCP connection to that program establishes that transport setup reached the server; it does not promise a PONG or a vote response.

The latest shared exchange had moved further, attempting to multiplex clients with poll(). Several mistakes made it a particularly useful exercise in state ownership and partial progress.

Connection establishment is not command arrival

The attempted accept branch registered a client for polling, then immediately called the old request handler and closed the client. On a nonblocking socket, there might not yet be request bytes. recv could return EAGAIN or EWOULDBLOCK; the old line reader treated any non-positive return as a reason to stop. The server could close before the client even sent its command.

Event What has been established What has not
accept succeeds A client connection is available A complete application command is present
Client is readable A receive attempt can make progress or reveal stream state A whole line necessarily fits in one read
recv returns bytes Those bytes are available They end at an application message boundary
Parser finds a newline One complete line is available under this protocol Every other connection is finished
send returns a positive count That many bytes were accepted by the call The entire response was sent or remotely processed

Registering a descriptor transfers it into the event loop's lifecycle. Closing it immediately while leaving its number in the poll array creates another problem: the table contains a descriptor that is no longer the live connection it claims to represent. Ignoring POLLNVAL can turn that into a busy or capacity-exhausted loop.

The attempted client branch also read bytes without feeding them to the command processor that generated replies. Two competing receive paths could therefore consume a request while neither produced the expected response. The fix begins with one receive path, one framing policy, and one place where complete commands change node state.

TCP preserves a byte stream, not your function calls

A client can send PI and later NG\n. It can also send PING\nSTATUS\n in one call. The server must preserve an incomplete first fragment and parse both complete lines in the second case. Neither behavior should require changing the business logic for PING.

Similarly, if an output call sends only part of the queued reply, the connection must retain the remainder and its offset. Retrying the entire reply from byte zero can duplicate a prefix. Treating a temporary inability to write as success can silently lose the suffix.

The useful per-client state is therefore more than an integer descriptor: input bytes and length, output bytes and length, a sent offset, and lifecycle information. The event loop owns this state across readiness notifications.

Nonblocking does not mean background completion

O_NONBLOCK means the operation can return instead of waiting when it cannot make progress. It does not arrange for C code to finish the operation later. poll reports readiness; the program must attempt I/O, interpret the result, and keep whatever state is needed for the next attempt.

For a stream receive of a nonzero requested length, distinguish:

Result Interpretation in this exercise
Positive count Append the bytes and look for complete frames
Zero Peer has ended its sending stream
-1, EINTR Retry according to the operation's policy
-1, EAGAIN/EWOULDBLOCK Keep connection state and wait for later readiness
Other error Apply the connection's error/close policy

Also, the third argument of poll is a timeout in milliseconds, not a port. The parsed listening port must actually be used when constructing the bind address. Both errors appeared in the latest debugging discussion and are separate from the framing problem.

A worked transport-only companion

The following optional teaching implementation demonstrates those mechanics without implementing peer elections or consensus. It accepts newline-delimited PING and STATUS, retains partial input/output, bounds its buffers, and uses one thread to own its request counter. It binds to loopback for a single-machine lab. Linux/WSL is the target; MSG_NOSIGNAL is used for sends.

This is newly written teaching code, not a claim that the source thread had already completed its nonblocking server. The submitted serving.c and the latest shared attempt remain archived separately.

poll_ping.c:

#define _POSIX_C_SOURCE 200809L
#include <arpa/inet.h>
#include <errno.h>
#include <fcntl.h>
#include <poll.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>
#include <string.h>
#include <sys/socket.h>
#include <unistd.h>

#define MAX_CLIENTS 16
#define INPUT_SIZE 1024
#define OUTPUT_SIZE 4096

struct Client {
    int fd;
    int peer_closed;
    char input[INPUT_SIZE];
    size_t input_len;
    char output[OUTPUT_SIZE];
    size_t output_len;
    size_t output_sent;
};

unsigned long requests = 0; // One event-loop thread owns this state

int make_nonblocking(int fd) {
    int flags = fcntl(fd, F_GETFL, 0);
    return flags < 0 ? -1 : fcntl(fd, F_SETFL, flags | O_NONBLOCK);
}

void close_client(struct Client *c) {
    if (c->fd >= 0) close(c->fd);
    *c = (struct Client){.fd = -1};
}

int queue_reply(struct Client *c, const char *line) {
    char reply[128];
    requests++;
    if (!strcmp(line, "PING")) snprintf(reply, sizeof(reply), "PONG\n");
    else if (!strcmp(line, "STATUS")) snprintf(reply, sizeof(reply), "requests=%lu\n", requests);
    else snprintf(reply, sizeof(reply), "ERR unknown command\n");
    size_t len = strlen(reply);
    if (len > sizeof(c->output) - c->output_len) return -1;
    memcpy(c->output + c->output_len, reply, len);
    c->output_len += len;
    return 0;
}

int read_client(struct Client *c) {
    for (;;) {
        size_t space = sizeof(c->input) - 1 - c->input_len;
        if (!space) return -1; // Bounded line: do not overrun or wait forever
        ssize_t n = recv(c->fd, c->input + c->input_len, space, 0);
        if (n > 0) {
            c->input_len += (size_t)n;
            c->input[c->input_len] = '\0';
            char *newline;
            while ((newline = memchr(c->input, '\n', c->input_len)) != NULL) {
                size_t used = (size_t)(newline - c->input) + 1;
                *newline = '\0';
                if (queue_reply(c, c->input) < 0) return -1;
                memmove(c->input, c->input + used, c->input_len - used);
                c->input_len -= used;
                c->input[c->input_len] = '\0';
            }
        } else if (n == 0) {
            c->peer_closed = 1;
            return 0; // Incomplete tail is discarded; complete replies still drain
        } else if (errno == EINTR) {
            continue;
        } else if (errno == EAGAIN || errno == EWOULDBLOCK) {
            return 0; // No bytes now; retain the partial line
        } else {
            return -1;
        }
    }
}

int write_client(struct Client *c) {
    while (c->output_sent < c->output_len) {
        ssize_t n = send(c->fd, c->output + c->output_sent, c->output_len - c->output_sent, MSG_NOSIGNAL);
        if (n > 0) c->output_sent += (size_t)n;
        else if (n < 0 && errno == EINTR) continue;
        else if (n < 0 && (errno == EAGAIN || errno == EWOULDBLOCK)) return 0;
        else return -1;
    }
    c->output_len = c->output_sent = 0;
    return 0;
}

int main(int argc, char **argv) {
    char *end;
    long port = argc == 2 ? strtol(argv[1], &end, 10) : 5050;
    if (argc > 2 || (argc == 2 && (!argv[1][0] || *end)) || port < 1 || port > 65535) {
        fprintf(stderr, "usage: %s [port:1..65535]\n", argv[0]);
        return 2;
    }
    int listener = socket(AF_INET, SOCK_STREAM, 0), one = 1;
    if (listener < 0) { perror("socket"); return 1; }
    struct sockaddr_in address = {.sin_family = AF_INET, .sin_addr.s_addr = htonl(INADDR_LOOPBACK), .sin_port = htons((unsigned short)port)};
    if (setsockopt(listener, SOL_SOCKET, SO_REUSEADDR, &one, sizeof(one)) < 0 ||
        make_nonblocking(listener) < 0 || bind(listener, (struct sockaddr *)&address, sizeof(address)) < 0 || listen(listener, MAX_CLIENTS) < 0) {
        perror("listener setup");
        close(listener);
        return 1;
    }
    struct Client clients[MAX_CLIENTS];
    for (int i = 0; i < MAX_CLIENTS; i++) clients[i] = (struct Client){.fd = -1};
    printf("Listening on 127.0.0.1:%ld\n", port);
    fflush(stdout);
    for (;;) {
        struct pollfd fds[MAX_CLIENTS + 1] = {{.fd = listener, .events = POLLIN}};
        for (int i = 0; i < MAX_CLIENTS; i++) {
            fds[i + 1].fd = clients[i].fd;
            fds[i + 1].events = clients[i].peer_closed ? 0 : POLLIN;
            if (clients[i].output_len > clients[i].output_sent) fds[i + 1].events |= POLLOUT;
        }
        int ready = poll(fds, MAX_CLIENTS + 1, -1);
        if (ready < 0) {
            if (errno == EINTR) continue;
            perror("poll");
            break;
        }
        if (fds[0].revents & POLLIN) {
            for (;;) {
                int fd = accept(listener, NULL, NULL);
                if (fd < 0) {
                    if (errno == EINTR) continue;
                    if (errno != EAGAIN && errno != EWOULDBLOCK) perror("accept");
                    break;
                }
                int slot;
                for (slot = 0; slot < MAX_CLIENTS && clients[slot].fd >= 0; slot++) {}
                if (slot == MAX_CLIENTS || make_nonblocking(fd) < 0) close(fd);
                else clients[slot] = (struct Client){.fd = fd};
            }
        }
        for (int i = 0; i < MAX_CLIENTS; i++) {
            struct Client *c = &clients[i];
            short events = fds[i + 1].revents;
            if (c->fd < 0 || !events) continue;
            if (events & (POLLERR | POLLNVAL)) { close_client(c); continue; }
            if ((events & (POLLIN | POLLHUP)) && read_client(c) < 0) { close_client(c); continue; }
            if (c->output_len > c->output_sent && write_client(c) < 0) { close_client(c); continue; }
            if (c->peer_closed && c->output_len == c->output_sent) close_client(c);
        }
    }
    for (int i = 0; i < MAX_CLIENTS; i++) close_client(&clients[i]);
    close(listener);
    return 1;
}

The request counter measures parsed commands in this local teaching server, including an unknown command that receives an error. The implementation closes connections on overlong lines or an exhausted output queue rather than overrunning storage; it discards an incomplete tail after peer EOF. These are explicit bounded teaching policies, not a general production protocol. It does not provide authenticated requests, replicated durable state, election terms, or authority to commit work.

Why one thread can service multiple clients

While one client has only supplied PI, another can supply a complete PING. The server need not block waiting for the first client's remaining bytes. One event-loop thread can keep both connections in progress and process complete commands one at a time. That eliminates simultaneous local writes to its ordinary request counter without eliminating network concurrency.

The original three machines still run independently. Once they exchange votes, this locally orderly implementation will face incomplete remote knowledge again. Transport correctness is the prerequisite that lets us study that next failure without confusing it with a dropped partial line.

Appendix B. Guided review with explained answers

These exercises ask for a reason, not only a yes/no result. Pause before each answer and identify the relevant state, observation, and acceptance boundary.

1. Two private locks around one counter

Problem: both workers call the same function. Inside it, each initializes a mutex, locks it, and increments a shared ordinary counter. Why is this not a repair?

Reasoning: the two invocations contain different mutex objects. The lock operations do not contend, so the counter accesses remain unordered by a common protocol. Share the coordination object and keep every relevant concurrent access inside its protocol. Sharing the function's source text is not sharing the mutex's identity.

2. Every access is atomic, yet two tickets are sold

Problem: both buyers load one available seat, then each stores zero and records a sale. What guarantee was missing?

Reasoning: the check and conditional change were split. Atomic loads and stores define each access; they do not make the premise remain true until the later write. A successful CAS can be the conditional claim, or a common mutex can cover the whole logical operation. Stronger ordering on the two separate accesses does not merge them into one operation.

3. A correct count and a misleading benchmark

Problem: a program increments separate counters but prints the other counter on every iteration. Does slow execution isolate false sharing?

Reasoning: no. The workload also contains cross-reading, formatting, stream synchronization, and output costs. Those affect both behavior and timing. Check counts, memory layout, and the hot-loop workload separately. A controlled same-work/layout comparison narrows the performance claim.

4. The ready flag is atomic; the payload is ordinary

Problem: the consumer sees ready == 1. When is reading the ordinary payload justified?

Reasoning: in the one-shot example, the consumer's acquire must observe the release publication that followed payload initialization. That creates the necessary ordering for the subsequent payload read. A relaxed observation alone does not provide it. A reused payload needs an additional ownership or lifetime protocol preventing concurrent overwrite.

5. An optimistic reservation fails

Problem: a worker saw a free ticket and later its strong CAS failed. Was an invalid ticket issued?

Reasoning: failure means this attempt did not acquire ownership. Rejecting that stale claim is how the invariant is maintained. Inspect where the program records a successful sale; it belongs only on the successful claim path. Another attempt or another customer operation is not automatically replay-safe if it has external effects.

6. Versions match around an ordinary racy read

Problem: a reader races on non-atomic fields, then checks that the version counter was unchanged. Does successful validation make the accesses legal C?

Reasoning: no. A post-read check cannot undo undefined behavior caused by the accesses themselves. The teaching snapshot uses atomic payload fields and a simple ordering model, then validates their relationship. A production seqlock-style implementation needs an argument appropriate to its language and platform mechanisms.

7. Three honest replicas split A/A/B

Problem: which replica should be considered untrustworthy because of this disagreement?

Reasoning: none can be identified from that fact alone. Different request arrival orders can make all three valid local decisions. The goal is one safe accepted proposal, not a diagnosis that the minority must be lying. To establish a protocol violation, we would need evidence of a violated rule, such as incompatible endorsements under rules that prohibit them.

8. A wins in one execution; B wins in another

Problem: does that show conflicting commits?

Reasoning: not for an invariant scoped to a single initialized decision instance. Independent runs may choose different allowed values. A conflicting commit claim needs incompatible decisions for the same instance under the same relevant history. A round number, a log position, and a process invocation are not interchangeable identifiers.

9. Coordinator Y timed out after two votes

Problem: what can Y conclude about whether coordinator X collected three?

Reasoning: Y cannot infer X's complete observation from Y's incomplete one. X may have the delayed vote. Recovery therefore needs rules preserving evidence that constrains safe new decisions, rather than treating silence as proof that no commitment exists.

10. An isolated old leader still accepts a matching-term job

Problem: is the old node's local equality test wrong?

Reasoning: it can correctly compare two stale local values. The missing contract is whether that local belief is sufficient authority to produce an accepted effect. A system may permit stale beliefs while preventing stale actors from obtaining a valid commit or passing the resource's fencing rule. Electing a new leader alone does not make every old belief vanish.

11. poll reports a readable client

Problem: may the handler assume one complete newline command will arrive in a single recv?

Reasoning: no. Readiness concerns the stream operation, not the application's message format. Accumulate available bytes, preserve incomplete frames across events, and parse complete frames according to the chosen protocol. A temporary EAGAIN is different from stream EOF.

12. Multiple active connections, one event-loop thread

Problem: must the request counter be atomic merely because two clients are connected?

Reasoning: not if one execution thread owns all its accesses and no asynchronous actor touches it. The connections are concurrent, but the handlers are serialized by that architecture. Independent remote nodes still operate concurrently; the local ownership model does not solve their agreement problem.
